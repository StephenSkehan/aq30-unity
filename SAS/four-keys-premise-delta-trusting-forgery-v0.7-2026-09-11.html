1. The device

Before (v8.1, ruled). A dead woman's accusation tears four friends apart. Her key letter: I think one of you is planning to kill me. The locker letter, forged: I know. Chapter 7: dead women don't get surer.

After (v0.7). A woman who trusted four people completely asked them, from behind a padlocked door, to find out who had been getting into her house. She said it was none of them. Within a month the four were sure it was one of them. Violet never accuses anyone. The accusation is built at Ruby's kitchen table by the four themselves, out of true things, and the true things were chosen by the one of them who killed her. Brad does not forge her fear. He forges her trust, keeps her voice and her instruction, salts the list so that four honest, frightened, competent people arrive at Liam, and writes one kind line about himself that only one person alive can know is false. What lets the four cross out her clearance is not a character reading. Everything downstream holds as ruled: the room lands on Liam, Margo keeps her silence, Brad vanishes off Kestrel Head on Day 60, Margo dies there on Day 214, the survivors accuse each other to the police, the police look properly and find nothing, Ruby talks to the Gazette, Ally begins.

2. Violet

Forty-four, the best judge of a hand on the coast, and she writes everything down, dated and timed. No will: a woman with a solicitor and death-triggered envelopes who kept meaning to make one, so her sister, as next of kin, will administer everything, which is the irony of the fight over their mother's house. She has money her friends know about and do not ask about, and she has been open-handed with it: eight years ago she set Brad up, the van and his first stock, in cash, on no paper, against Margo's advice. All four know that. What none of them but Brad and Margo know is that this autumn she started calling it in, for the lawyer in the fight with her sister: the solicitor's account for the hearing falls due at the month's end, and she told Brad on Day −110 she wanted the van money by then. She said at the café once, in passing and without a name, that she was calling in what she was owed.

This autumn, two things inside her own house. Her tobacco, two a day rolled from the tin under the stairs among the paints and the garden concentrates, comes up stained through the paper; the lid is loose; the capped bottle on the shelf above has moved six inches or has not. She bins the tobacco that day and does not smoke from that tin again. She moves the house spare from the meter box to the second pot by the yard door, and tells the four on the slip, in one sentence, without saying why. And a gilded alphabet panel comes down out of her workshop roof with a ceiling board: her own alphabet, better than she has ever done it, signed with a mark that is not a name.

She carries the panel round the trade asking whose hand it is: the café, the cinema, two other signwriters. Two say yours, in a very good year, and one asks the obvious question: have you shown your lad? She shows him last, on the morning of Day −19, at the sheds, with the panel under her arm on its way into her store. He looks at it a long time and says yours, and names the R. Three yours. What she tells herself is that it cannot be his because she never taught him leaf and he never asked; what she writes is that everyone says it is hers and she did not make it. The other three never see it: they are not lettering people. Nobody in town knows Brad can gild; his boat names are painted, the leaf work at the yard was always hers, and his leaf and size come by post to his flat.

She believes someone with her spare key has been in her house. She also believes, on alternate days, that she is imagining it. She does not suspect any of the four for a second, and the reason she does not call them is not fear of them: she has nothing to show them but a loose lid and a panel three people have told her is her own, and she would rather be laughed at after she is gone than watched while she is here. So she writes it down. Her store at the slip boatsheds is one of the old brick boat stores: a bay, a steel door on a shrouded hasp, nothing visible from outside, where she kept her hull kit. It is paid a year ahead in cash to the market manager, who lets forty bays for cash to people who do not want questions asked and asks none; no receipt, and in no ledger Margo ever saw. She moves the kit to the van. The morning of Day −19, in her book with times: four keys cut on Lantern Street at nine, the packet's two dropped off the end of the slip; the panel, the letter and her mother into the store and the closed-shackle marine padlock on at ten, with Brad watching; the four keys into four envelopes at Civic Row before noon, to be posted after her funeral. He asks; she makes a joke about her sister. The keys are never in her house. Then she gets on, calmer, because it is out of her head. She does not expect it to be needed.

3. The three letters, sketched (Fable writes prose, blind)

The key letter (genuine; four identical copies; posted Saturday Day 12; three by home delivery, one to a post office box; the solicitor's slip in each lists the four addressees as instructed). If you've got this, I'm dead, and I'm sorry about the timing. Go to my store at the boatsheds by the slip. The padlock's new and these four keys are the only ones. Go together, all four of you, and read what's there together. Violet. Nothing else.

The locker letter (genuine; dated Day −19; Brad reads it at 07:20 on Day 14; burned in his own grate that evening with his drafts and her book). It isn't any of you four, and I'd know. Someone with my key has been in my house, or I have started imagining things, and I cannot tell which, and both frighten me. Only you four know where the spare lives, and I've moved it once already. The tin: the two a day she lies about, the stain through the paper, the loose lid, the bottle on the shelf above; I don't know whether I did that; I've thrown the tobacco out. The panel: the leak, the board, her own alphabet done better than she can do it, a mark for a name; Brad says it's mine, everyone says it's mine, I didn't make it; and if I didn't, somebody has been in my roof. Mum, on the shelf in the back bedroom, facing the door one morning when I leave her facing the window; I've put her in here. Then: each of you write down everything odd you've noticed this year, about each other and about me, however small, and read the four lists together at one table. Then the police, with something in your hands. Write legibly. Nothing about money: the calling-in is not odd to her, it is a lawyer's bill. Brad reads it and knows what the room never will: pooled, the odd things about him name a man preparing to leave, and the panel names a gilder.

The locker letter (forged; dated Day −19 as she dated everything; what the four read at nine that night). Her paper and her pen, taken from the workshop on Day −9 with nothing else; drafted from Day −9 at his own bench from what her book said she had noticed, and conformed to her true letter on the morning of Day 14, at his bench, in two hours. Her frame, kept: it isn't any of you four, and I'd know; someone with my key has been in my house, or I'm imagining it, which frightens me more; only you four know where the spare lives. Her reason for the scale, in her voice: I don't think I'm going to die. I think I'm going to be sixty and embarrassed. But I'm putting this where it can't be got at, because if I'm wrong nobody needs to know, and if I'm right you'll want to know what I noticed. If I'd told you now you'd have sat in my kitchen with a torch and I'd never have heard the end of it. The object: I have put Mum in here with this. One morning last month she was facing the door, and I leave her facing the window. Somebody has been in the back bedroom, or I have started moving things and forgetting. Either way she is not staying on a shelf for whoever it is to stand and look at. Keep her where all four of you can see her until this is sorted. Her instruction, kept. Then, "since I'm asking you to, here's mine", bare fact only, nothing she was not present for: Liam has owned a third of the yard for eight years and lets the town think he's staff. Liam asked in March and again in July to take Mum out to the point on his boat; I said no both times. Ruby has been on nights since August and won't say why. And the one kind thing, which is the structural change this round: Brad paid me back the van money in October, every penny, which I never asked him to do that fast, and I put it here because it is the least odd thing that happened this year and I wanted one nice thing on the list. His one addition: not the police. I've nothing they'd act on and I won't have them in your lives over a shelf. You four can do what they won't. Her close, kept: read this together, be careful, write legibly.

What he cut: the tin (a poisoning in her hand sends a nurse to the police through any ban) and the panel (it names a gilder). What he kept that he could have cut: her sentence about the spare, because it is true and it closes the room on itself. What he added: the ban, three true things that lean, and one lie that only one living person can know is a lie. The lean is in the choosing. The lie is the only thing in the letter that is not true, and it is about him, and it is kind.

4. Brad before the night, on the night, and the fourteen days

Day −19. He watches her lock his panel into her store and drive off with the keys. He knows what she has found and that she writes things down. Day −9. With the spare, while she is at the slip, he reads her book of everything: the store, the padlock, the keys cut and lodged and the packet's two in the harbour, the four envelopes, the tin, the shelf, the calling-in and the date. He takes a sheet of her layout paper and her pen from the workshop. The plan is made in knowledge, which is this man. Day 0, the last Monday before the month's end. He comes about the money; she pours two glasses; she goes up for the book to show him the figure; the landing. In the twenty minutes he waits he takes the book, because it is his exemplar and the only record of what he owes. He rinses his glass and puts it on the rack; hers stays on the side. Out by the back door at 22:30, lifted and pulled so it catches; the neighbour at №14 sees a man's back, a coat, hands empty; the book is under the coat. He is never in her house again.

He does not go near the store for fourteen days, and it is the least-cost choice for this man. Nobody else can open it before the keys arrive: no fifth key exists, the packet's keys are in the harbour, the keys were never in her house, the store is in no record the sister receives, and a brick bay with a steel door on a shrouded hasp has no partition, roof or hinge to go through. A cut, picked or forced lock is the one event in the story that makes the police look. The sheds are worked from first light, so an early visit has the same witness on any day and a torch at two in the morning is what gets remembered. His own locker in the same corridor is a reason to be there at seven, and his PO box gives him seven. From Day −9 he drafts at his own bench. On Day 14 at 07:20 he opens her store, reads her true letter, takes it and the panel to his own locker, and drives home. The boat owner saw a signwriter at her store door at twenty past seven, carrying a board between two doors, and thought her pupil was collecting her kit.
