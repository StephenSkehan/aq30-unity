## 1. The device

**Before (v8.1, ruled).** A dead woman's accusation tears four friends apart. Her key letter: I think one of you is planning to kill me. The locker letter, forged: I know. Chapter 7: dead women don't get surer.

**After (v0.8).** A woman who trusted four people completely asked them, from behind a padlocked door, to find out who had been getting into her house. She said it was none of them. She had changed her lock and told only the four where the new spare lived, and it had happened again after, so she knew what the arithmetic said, and she wrote down that she would rather be imagining it. Within a month the four were sure it was one of them. Violet never accuses anyone. The accusation is built at Ruby's kitchen table by the four themselves, out of true things, and the true things were chosen by the one of them who killed her, who also wrote one kind line about himself that only one person alive can know is false.

Everything downstream holds as ruled: the room lands on Liam, Margo keeps her silence, Brad vanishes off Kestrel Head on Day 60, Margo dies there on Day 214, the survivors accuse each other to the police, the police look properly and find nothing, Ruby talks to the Gazette, Ally begins.

## 2. Violet

Forty-four, the best judge of a hand on the coast, and she writes everything down, dated and timed. No will, so her sister, as next of kin, will administer everything, which is the irony of the fight over their mother's house. She has money her friends know about and do not ask about, and she has been open-handed with it: eight years ago she set Brad up, the van and his first stock, in cash, on no paper, against Margo's advice. All four know that. What none of them but Brad and Margo know is that this autumn she started calling it in, for the lawyer in the fight with her sister: the solicitor's account for the hearing falls due at the month's end, she told Brad on Day −110 she wanted the van money by then, and she told him what she would do on the first of the month if it had not come: have Margo write it up properly and start asking where the van money had gone. She said at the café once, in passing and without a name, that she was calling in what she was owed.

This autumn, inside her own house. Her tobacco, two a day rolled from the tin under the stairs among the paints and the garden concentrates, comes up stained through the paper; the lid is loose; the capped bottle on the shelf above has moved six inches or has not. She bins the tobacco that day. **Day −37, the day after, she changes the front door lock (structural change this round)**, has one new spare cut, puts it in the second pot by the yard door, and tells the four on the slip, in one sentence, because the spare is for them: Liam has let himself in to check on her for twenty years. She does not say why. Then, in the weeks after the change, her mother, on the shelf in the back bedroom, is facing the door one morning when she leaves her facing the window. That is the trap she writes from. After the lock, five people knew the pot, and one is her. So either it is one of the four, which she cannot believe, or she is imagining it, which she would rather. To tell them is to accuse them. So she writes it down, for after. And a gilded alphabet panel comes down out of her workshop roof with a ceiling board: her own alphabet, better than she has ever done it, signed with a mark that is not a name.

She carries the panel round the trade asking whose hand it is: the café, the cinema, two other signwriters. Two say yours, in a very good year, and one asks the obvious question: have you shown your lad? She shows him last, on the morning of Day −19, at the sheds, with the panel under her arm on its way into her store. He looks at it a long time and says yours, and names the R. Three yours. What she tells herself is that it cannot be his because she never taught him leaf and he never asked; what she writes is that everyone says it is hers and she did not make it. The other three never see it. Nobody in town knows Brad can gild; his boat names are painted, the leaf work at the yard was always hers, and his leaf and size come by post to his box.

Her store at the slip boatsheds is one of the old brick boat stores: a bay, a steel door on a shrouded hasp, nothing visible from outside, where she kept her hull kit. Paid a year ahead in cash to the market manager, who lets forty bays for cash to people who do not want questions asked and asks none; no receipt; in no ledger Margo ever saw. She moves the kit to the van. **The morning of Day −19, in her book with times:** four keys cut on Lantern Street at nine, the packet's two dropped off the end of the slip; the panel, the letter and her mother into the store and the closed-shackle marine padlock on at ten, with Brad watching; the four keys into four envelopes at Civic Row before noon, addressed as she addresses them, Brad's to the box that is on his van, to be posted after her funeral. He asks; she makes a joke about her sister. The keys are never in her house. Then she gets on, calmer, because it is out of her head. She does not expect it to be needed.

## 3. The three letters, sketched (Fable writes prose, blind)

**The key letter (genuine; four identical copies; posted Saturday Day 12; three by home delivery, one to a post office box; the solicitor's slip in each lists the four addressees as instructed).** If you've got this, I'm dead, and I'm sorry about the timing. Go to my store at the boatsheds by the slip. The padlock's new and these four keys are the only ones. Go together, all four of you, and read what's there together. Violet. Nothing else.

**The locker letter (genuine; dated Day −19; Brad reads it at 07:20 on Day 14; burned in his own grate that evening with his drafts and her book).** It isn't any of you four, and I'd know. Someone with my key has been in my house, or I have started imagining things, and I cannot tell which, and both frighten me. I changed the lock in October and told only you four where the new spare lives, and it happened again after, so I know what that says, and I would rather be losing my mind. The tin: the two a day she lies about, the stain through the paper, the loose lid, the bottle on the shelf above; I don't know whether I did that; I've thrown the tobacco out. The panel: the leak, the board, her own alphabet done better than she can do it, a mark for a name; Brad says it's mine, everyone says it's mine, I didn't make it; and if I didn't, somebody has been in my roof. Mum, facing the door; I've put her in here. Then her instruction, with her reason in it: somebody got the place of my spare from one of you without your knowing, or watched one of you use it; so write down everything odd this year, about me, and about who has been near each of you, asking, or hanging about, or in your houses; and read the four lists together at one table. Then the police, with something in your hands. Write legibly. Nothing about money: the calling-in is a lawyer's bill, not an oddity. Brad reads it and knows what the room never will: pooled, the odd things about him name a man preparing to leave, and the panel names a gilder.

**The locker letter (forged; dated Day −19 as she dated everything; what the four read at nine that night).** Her paper and her pen, taken from the workshop on Day −9 with nothing else; drafted from Day −9 at his own bench from what her book said she had noticed, and conformed to her true letter on the morning of Day 14, at his bench, in two hours. Her frame, kept: it isn't any of you four, and I'd know; someone with my key has been in my house, or I'm imagining it, which frightens me more; I changed the lock in October and told only you four where the new spare lives, and it happened again after. Her reason for the scale, in her voice: I don't think I'm going to die. I think I'm going to be sixty and embarrassed. But I'm putting this where it can't be got at, because if I'm wrong nobody needs to know, and if I'm right you'll want to know what I noticed. If I'd told you now you'd have sat in my kitchen with a torch and I'd never have heard the end of it. The object: I have put Mum in here with this. One morning last month she was facing the door, and I leave her facing the window. Somebody has been in the back bedroom, or I have started moving things and forgetting. Either way she is not staying on a shelf for whoever it is to stand and look at. Keep her where all four of you can see her until this is sorted. Her instruction, kept with its reason: somebody got the place of my spare from one of you without your knowing; write down everything odd this year, about me, and about who has been near each of you, and read it together at one table. Then, "since I'm asking you to, here's mine", bare fact only, nothing she was not present for: Liam has owned a third of the yard for eight years and lets the town think he's staff. Liam asked in March and again in July to take Mum out to the point on his boat; I said no both times. Ruby has been on nights since August and won't say why. **And the one kind thing:** Brad paid me back the van money in October, all of it, out of the blue, and I put it here because it is the least odd thing that happened this year and I wanted one nice thing on the list. His one addition: not the police. I've nothing they'd act on and I won't have them in your lives over a shelf. You four can do what they won't. Her close, kept: read this together, be careful, write legibly.

What he cut: the tin and the panel. What he kept that he could have cut: the lock, the pot and the four, because it is true and it closes the room on itself. What he added: the ban, three true things that lean, and one lie that only one living person can know is a lie, about him, and kind.

## 4. Brad before the night, on the night, and the fourteen days

**Why he cannot walk.** His exit is not a departure. It is a death. The deckhand's papers are for a man presumed drowned, because a living Brad Collins with a cash debt to a woman who forgets nobody is a man who is looked for, into a cash economy Margo audits, with a panel question still live in the trade; a dead one is grieved and forgotten. A death needs a grief the town will believe. Hers is that grief. And the month's end is her date, not his: on the first she has Margo write the loan up and starts asking where the van money went, which is the one conversation his exit cannot survive.

**Day −19.** He watches her lock his panel into her store and drive off with the keys. **Day −9.** With the new spare from the second pot, while she is at the slip, he reads her book of everything: the lock, the pot, the store, the keys cut and lodged and the packet's two in the harbour, the four envelopes, the tin, the shelf, the calling-in and the date. He takes a sheet of her layout paper and her pen from the workshop. The plan is made in knowledge. **Day 0, the last Monday before the month's end.** He comes about the money; she pours two glasses; she goes up for the book to show him the figure; the landing. In the twenty minutes he waits he takes the book. He rinses his glass and puts it on the rack; hers stays on the side. Out by the back door at 22:30, lifted and pulled so it catches; the neighbour at №14 sees a man's back, a coat, hands empty; the book is under the coat. He is never in her house again.

**Day 1 (canon).** Liam lets himself in with the spare from the second pot at two, finds her, rings. Uniform, then Del: unwitnessed, head injury, foot of the stairs, front locked, back latched, no disturbance, one glass on the side; coroner's officer informed; door-knock: №14 saw her Monday morning and a man's back in the lane about half ten, a coat, nothing in his hands; noted, unidentified male, nothing to identify him against. **Day 12 (canon).** Her solicitor, informed of the death, posts the four envelopes as instructed; he does not know their contents; he will confirm the lodging to anyone entitled to ask and volunteers nothing to the other side's solicitor beyond what the administration requires.

**The fourteen days.** He does not go near the store, and it is the least-cost choice: nobody else can open it before the keys arrive (no fifth key; the packet's keys in the harbour; the keys never in her house; the store in no record the sister receives; a brick bay with no partition, roof or hinge to go through), and a cut or picked lock is the one event that makes the police look. The sheds are worked from first light; his own locker in the same corridor is a reason to be there at seven; his box gives him seven. From Day −9 he drafts at his own bench. On Day 14 at 07:20 he opens her store, reads her true letter, takes it and the panel to his own locker, and drives home. By ten the draft is conformed; by two the forgery and her mother are in her store; that evening the drafts, the true letter and the book go into his own grate. The boat owner saw a signwriter at her store door at twenty past seven, carrying a board between two doors, and thought her pupil was collecting her kit.
