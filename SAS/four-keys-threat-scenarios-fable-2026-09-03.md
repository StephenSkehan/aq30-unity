**1. The back door and the cookie tin**

She comes in late on a Thursday and goes to lock the front door as she does every night, turning the key and leaving it in the inside of the lock so she can get out fast. The key is not in the lock and not on its hook. She finds it forty minutes later in the cookie tin in the kitchen cupboard, under the cash, the one place in the house that has never held a key. In the morning she goes to put the bins out through the back and the door will not move: two screw heads, countersunk through the door edge into the frame, painted over in the door's own blue from the touch-up tin under the sink.

It needed the house key (Liam holds one, and all four know the meter-box spare), the knowledge that she sleeps in the back and keeps the key in the front lock at night, and the tin. Nobody outside the four knows the tin exists.

The paint is what says deliberate. Paint does not dry over screws on its own, and the skin on her touch-up tin has been broken and pressed back. The screws are the long ones from her own jar. And the key was put in the tin, not dropped there: it was under the notes, not on them.

To anyone else there is no forced entry, no theft, her own screws in her own door under her own paint, her own key in her own tin. The officer would say, not unkindly, "Nothing's been taken? Are you sleeping all right, Ms Moore?" There is no offence in screwing your own door shut.

She can photograph the screw heads before she takes them out, keep the two screws and the touch-up tin, and wrap and label the key.

The failure: the fire it is a rehearsal for never arrives on the page, so the reader must take the death on trust, and screwing a door shut is the closest of the ten to something an officer might actually write down.

**2. The kettle**

She walks up from the slip to the unit with sea-wet hair and hands and does what she always does before her coat is off: fills the kettle and puts her palm on it to click the switch. It bites her, a hard buzz up the forearm, and the consumer unit trips. She is standing on the wet patch she makes every morning. Her arm is numb for an hour and she works one-handed till lunch.

It needed the unit key (Brad holds one; the spare hangs on the hook in her hall, so anyone with the house key has it) and the knowledge that she puts a wet hand on a metal kettle within a minute of arriving. Only people who have been in that unit at a quarter past seven know that.

She cuts the plug open. The earth wire has been cut square, the copper bright, folded back beside its terminal with the screw done down on nothing. The clamp screws show fresh metal where they were turned. She rewired that plug herself in March and knows how she left it. Wear frays and blackens; it does not cut square and tuck itself away.

To anyone else it is a cheap kettle in a damp unit with a home-wired plug. The officer would say, "Wires work loose. You had a shock and no harm done. What would you like me to do?"

She can keep the plug, cut off with the wire folded exactly as she found it, in a bag, and photographs of the inside before she touched it.

The failure: it is an electrician's argument, and if the reader needs a paragraph on why a missing earth kills a wet woman, the fear leaks out through the paragraph.

**3. The tide table**

At a quarter to six on a Wednesday in October she stands at the kitchen wall with her tea and reads the tide table pinned there, as she does, then walks to the slip. The water is wrong. The table said slack low at six; the harbour is running hard on a big ebb, the moorings streaming. She does not go in. She stands on the slip and knows the table lied.

It needed the house key and the knowledge that she plans every swim from that booklet on that wall. The four have watched her do it.

At home she takes the booklet down. It is this year's, same chandlery, same cover, but for the port forty minutes up the coast, and the printed port name has been over-lettered with "V. MOORE  HAVENBAY" in her own block capitals, as she letters everything she owns. Except it is not her hand. The six is her six, but the crossbar on the H is heavy and the spacing is copied, not felt. She ranks handwriting out loud; she knows a copy the way a singer knows a wrong note. The paint is hers, from the unit.

To anyone else it is her writing on her booklet. The officer would say, "You picked up the wrong one at the chandlers and signed it. You're a signwriter and you'd know, but I can't take that to anyone. There's no offence in a tide table."

She can keep the booklet and letter the same words on a card beside it in her own hand, so the four can compare. One of them will see the difference and know she saw it.

The failure: it asks the sea to do the killing, and a reader who knows she reads water will say she was never in danger. The scenario stands or falls on whether the forged lettering alone can carry the fear.

**4. The flue damper and the alarm**

After the swim she banks the stove in the front room and sits in front of it with the door shut and a towel on her head, as she does for an hour every night from October. The room smells sour and the fire is sulky. She opens the stove door and smoke rolls out into the room instead of up. She opens the window and kills the fire. Later she presses the test button on the carbon monoxide alarm on the mantel. Nothing.

It needed the house key and the knowledge that she spends that hour alone in that shut room every evening. The four have sat in that room while she did it.

The flue pipe has a damper she has never used; the handle was painted shut when she moved in. The paint round the spindle is cracked, the spindle has fresh grease on it, and the plate sits three-quarters closed with the handle turned back to the position that always looked open. Inside the alarm the battery is in place with a strip of clear blister plastic between it and the contact. Grease and plastic do not arrive on their own.

To anyone else a stove needs sweeping, dampers close, and everyone silences a chirping alarm and forgets. The officer would say, "Get a sweep in and a new alarm. There's no crime here."

She can keep the plastic strip and the alarm, and photograph the greased spindle before she wipes it.

The failure: two tamperings in one room read as an author stacking the deck, and carbon monoxide is the most familiar quiet murder on television.

**5. The window restrictor**

She sleeps with the back bedroom sash open a hand's width all year, above the flat roof of the kitchen. On a warm Sunday afternoon she pushes it higher and it runs freely to the top, where it never has. The brass restrictor bar she fitted herself is gone. Two screw holes have been filled with putty and painted in her own white.

It needed the house key and the knowledge that she sleeps with that particular window open, over that roof, even in November. Only people who have been upstairs in that house know it.

Filled and painted holes are the detail. Putty has to be pushed and paint has to be matched; nothing falls off a window and heals its own holes. She fitted that bar four years ago and the receipt is in the drawer.

To anyone else nothing has been taken and nobody has come in. The officer would say, "You're telling me a window opens? Perhaps you took it off for the painters." There is no offence.

She can photograph the filled holes and keep the receipt.

The failure: nothing was tried, so the fear is inferred from an absence, and four filled screw holes may not carry a reader all the way to "one of them means to come in while I sleep."

**6. The car in the yard**

Her car lives in the yard behind the house, gate padlocked, padlock key on the hall hook. After every swim she sits in it with the engine running and the heater on full for twenty minutes, windows shut, shivering herself warm, alone most mornings and with Ruby on Tuesdays. One Thursday the cabin fills with exhaust in minutes and she gets out thick-headed and stands in the yard till it clears.

It needed the house key for the padlock key, and the knowledge of the twenty minutes. The four have sat in the car with her or watched her from the kitchen window.

Under the driver's footwell the rubber grommet where the loom passes through the floor is out, lying whole in the undertray, not perished, and the exhaust joint below it has its clamp bolt backed out four turns with bright threads. Grommets crumble; they do not step out whole and lie down next to a loosened bolt.

To anyone else it is an old car and a rusty exhaust. The officer would say, "Get it serviced. I can't put a name to a loose bolt."

She can keep the grommet and photograph the bright threads before she tightens them.

The failure: a mechanic's report would actually help her, and the moment help is possible the loneliness of the premise weakens.

**7. The electric blanket controller**

On the first cold night in October she takes the underblanket out of the airing cupboard where it has lain since April. Before she plugs it in she looks at it, because she looks at everything. The controller is not hers. Hers had a fleck of signal red on the dial from a job in 2022. This one is the same make but an older model with no thermostat and a setting hers never had.

It needed the house key and the knowledge that she runs the blanket from October to March and packs it away the rest of the year. It was put there in summer to wait.

Present that should be absent: a controller that fits her blanket, works, and would have run unchecked all night under a woman who sleeps hard after cold water. Her own is nowhere in the house.

To anyone else she has muddled two blankets or a friend swapped one years ago. The officer would say, "It's a blanket. Throw it out."

She can keep the wrong controller, and if there is an old photograph of the bedroom with the red fleck showing, that too.

The failure: an electric blanket fire is a slow, uncertain death, and a reader may not believe a killer would bet on it.

**8. The sash window**

Every morning she pushes up the front bedroom sash and leans out over the sill to read the sea before she decides to swim. One morning the window is already six inches up, propped on one of her own paint stir sticks, which she has never done. She pulls the stick out to close it and the sash drops like a blade onto the sill.

It needed the house key and the knowledge that she leans out of that window at that hour. Only people who have woken up in that house, or been on the phone to her at six, know it.

Both sash cords hang slack in the frame, cut square across, not frayed to the rat's tail a worn cord leaves. She paints on fibre; she knows what cut looks like. The stick is from her kitchen drawer.

To anyone else sash cords go and somebody propped a window, probably her. The officer would say, "No one's hurt and it's your window."

She can keep the cut cord ends and the stick and photograph the frame.

The failure: the falling sash is a period-thriller device, and a window across the neck sits close enough to the forbidden fall that a reader will feel the repetition even if the rule is technically kept.

**9. The sign above the bench**

The old cast-iron brewery sign that hangs above the spot where she stands to eat every day at half twelve is a degree off level. She cannot not see a crooked line. She reaches up to true it and it comes away in her hands, forty pounds of iron, and she has to sit down on the floor of her own unit.

It needed the unit key or the spare on her hall hook, and the knowledge of exactly where she stands. The four have eaten standing at that bench with her.

Of the two coach screws one is absent altogether, not on the floor, not in the batten, and the other is backed out to its last two threads. A screw that works loose leaves a rust ring and lies where it fell. A hole with clean bright thread and no screw anywhere was emptied by a hand.

To anyone else it is an old fixing in old timber. The officer would say, "You're a tradeswoman. You know things come off walls."

She can keep the remaining screw and the sign, and photograph the empty hole.

The failure: it is a falling object, and though she does not fall the reader will file it with ladders anyway; it is also the one death that most plausibly happens by wear.

**10. The paraffin can**

Late on a Thursday, shutter down, she lights the paraffin heater at her feet as she does from October. It goes up yellow and roaring instead of blue, throws soot, and she smothers it with the fire blanket. She fills it from a five-litre can she marks with a paint line each time, because she likes to know her burn rate.

It needed the unit key and the knowledge of the late nights alone with the heater. The paint lines are known to anyone who has stood in the unit and asked what they were for.

The fuel stands an inch above her last line and she has not filled it. Under the paraffin it smells of petrol. Somebody topped it up.

To anyone else it is contaminated fuel from a garage. The officer would say, "Your own can in your own workshop. Complain to the forecourt."

She can keep a jar of the fuel and the can with its lines, and photograph both.

The failure: a lab would find the petrol in a day and settle the question, and once it can be settled the premise (she knows and cannot prove) dissolves. It is also the one scenario where "she filled it and forgot" is what the reader will think too.

**Ranking**

1. The back door and the cookie tin. It beats the kettle because it needs no fault to be believed, only two screws and a key, and the tin makes the four the only suspects without her having to reason at all.
2. The kettle. It beats the tide table because it touched her, and the plug is a thing she can seal in an envelope with her own rewiring date on it.
3. The tide table. It beats the damper because it makes her one skill the only instrument that can see the crime, and the officer's line "that's your writing" is rule six spoken aloud.
4. The flue damper and the alarm. It beats the window restrictor because it has a mechanism that would have killed her in her own chair without a mark, which is what a careful intimate would choose.
5. The window restrictor. It beats the car because it needs a house key and nothing else, so it points inward hardest of the ten.
6. The car in the yard. It beats the blanket because it puts Ruby's Tuesday inside the frame, which keeps all four alive as suspects rather than only the key-holders.
7. The electric blanket controller. It beats the sash because its patience, planted in June for October, is more frightening than any rig, and there is no fall in it.
8. The sash window. It beats the sign because the propping stick is her own object turned against her and the window is in the house, the more private room.
9. The sign above the bench. It beats the paraffin because the crooked-line detection is pure Violet, even if the mechanism is a falling object.
10. The paraffin can. Last because petrol in paraffin is the one detail a lab would settle in a day, which breaks rule six the moment she thinks of it.
