## 1. Violet’s engine

**Strongest objection:** Violet behaves as though she expects her death to be connected to the intrusion while claiming she does not seriously believe an intrusion occurred. A loose lid and an unexplained panel might justify making notes. They do not justify changing a boatshed padlock, cutting exactly four keys, constructing a posthumous evidence cache and engaging a solicitor to activate it after her funeral. That is a dead-man mechanism. It presupposes both danger and the possibility that she will not be alive to explain it.

Embarrassment does not close the gap. Violet is already willing to expose the panel to café staff, cinema staff and two professional peers. Those people can damage her reputation. Brad cannot. He is the trusted pupil best qualified to recognise her technique and identify another hand. Claiming that pride permits disclosure to strangers but prohibits disclosure to the trusted expert reads as deliberate removal of the witness who would solve the panel.

The machinery also creates the question the premise cannot answer: if Violet can visit a solicitor and describe enough to arrange four posthumous notices, why can she not confidentially tell that solicitor what is happening while she is alive?

**Severity:** Fatal.

**What would have to be true:** Violet would need an already established pattern of preparing posthumous contingencies for minor uncertainties, an independently demonstrated terror of Brad seeing professional decline, and a credible reason that the solicitor could hold envelopes but could not hear or preserve the underlying concern. None is present.

## 2. The Monday-night room and the following weeks

**The unanswered Monday-night question:** “Who had Violet’s house key, and where is it now?”

That is the first question competent adults ask after reading that someone entered with a key. They inventory keys, former holders, tradespeople, family access and missing copies. They do not begin a weekly character audit of four people whom Violet explicitly cleared.

The inward turn is not earned. The case against Liam consists of concealed boatyard ownership, one legitimate bedroom visit and two offers to help scatter ashes. None connects him to the ninth, the key, the panel, the poisoning or Violet’s death. His explanation for the boat requests is ordinary and available immediately. His refusal to press Violet after she said no is evidence of respecting her boundary, not breaching it.

The group must also ignore the document selectively. They trust Violet enough to obey its elaborate instructions, but disregard its clearest factual judgement: “It isn’t any of you four, and I’d know.” If they decide she could be wrong about Liam, they must also question her recollections, her starter list and her belief that anyone entered. The delta never stages that epistemic collapse. It simply makes everyone cross the sentence out without admitting it.

The timing is internally unstable. The first-minute claim says they were sure within three weeks. Meeting 3 occurs nineteen days after the reading, when “nobody says it.” The room does not formally land on Liam until Day 47, thirty-three days after the reading.

**Severity:** Fatal.

**What would have to be true:** The key inquiry would have to be visibly exhausted, Liam would need evidence connecting him to the dated intrusion rather than merely to the room months earlier, and the group would have to confront and resolve the fact that accusing him means declaring Violet wrong. The present three facts cannot do that.

## 3. Margo

Margo’s “reading” is not strong enough to support either her silence or Brad’s lethal response. An omitted debt, a missing book and a debtor’s silence can justify suspicion. They cannot justify privately reversing course in a murder-adjacent inquiry, withholding estate information and making herself vulnerable without creating a record.

Her earliest competent action is **Day 8**, when she discovers the debt book is missing. The recipient is **Violet’s executor or the solicitor handling her affairs**, not merely the sister in a casual conversation. Margo has Violet’s dated list identifying Brad and the amount. A missing financial record and an outstanding debt are estate-administration matters. Client confidentiality does not require concealing them from the client’s legal personal representative.

What happens is straightforward: the missing book is formally recorded, Margo secures her copy, Brad’s debt enters the estate administration, and the solicitor or executor contacts him. The financial fact is no longer confined to Brad and Margo. The load-bearing six-week silence disappears before the four even complete their first meeting.

There is a second failure on Day 51. If Margo believes the forged letter has deliberately omitted evidence pointing to Brad, she does not merely stop arguing at Meeting 6. Withdrawal silently communicates suspicion to the very man she suspects while protecting nobody. Brad then kills her because he supposedly understands the evidential meaning of her silence as precisely as she does. Both characters must treat an ambiguous omission as proof while refusing every competent protective action.

**Severity:** Fatal.

**What would have to be true:** The debt would have to fall outside any estate or accounting responsibility, Margo would need a credible legal or personal prohibition against telling the executor or solicitor, and Brad would need observable evidence that she had identified the forgery rather than merely changed her opinion about Liam.

## 4. Chapter 7

The omission is an inference, not a turn.

The forged letter does not claim to contain every odd fact. It gives a starter list “to show you what I mean” and instructs the four to compile the complete lists. The absence of the panel and debts therefore contradicts nothing the document claims about its own completeness.

The proposed turn also contains an internal factual contradiction. Section 4 calls the debts “known to the town.” Section 4’s Margo ruling says only Brad and Margo know they were called in. Both cannot be true.

If the panel and debts genuinely were the town’s two most conspicuous facts, Ruby or Liam could recognise their absence on Monday night. If they were not known to Ruby and Liam, the delta cannot invoke their public obviousness as proof of deliberate omission.

Ally can see the inference before Chapter 7. The delta expressly says she holds the letter and both witness streams by Chapter 6. Nothing new happens in Chapter 7 that makes the comparison possible. The reveal is being withheld by chapter placement, not by access.

The listed consequences do not rescue it. Four genuine notes show that the locker letter was the only document available for substitution, not that it was substituted. The ashes do not prove a forger entered the house because Violet could have moved them herself. A letter being “kind” to Brad is interpretation. None converts absence into authorship.

**Severity:** Fatal.

**What would have to be true:** The letter would have to represent its starter list as exhaustive, Ruby and Liam would need no earlier access to the omitted facts, and Ally would need to acquire the final necessary fact only at the Chapter 7 turn. The current sequence establishes the opposite.

## 5. The first minute

This is a summary, not a dramatic hook.

“She might be imagining it; she wasn’t; she was dead” compresses premise information and relies on narration to declare that an intrusion occurred. Her death does not itself prove she was not imagining the earlier anomalies. “Three weeks later they were sure it was one of them” reports the outcome of an investigation instead of placing the listener inside a dangerous moment.

The listener holds a retrospective puzzle: who entered, who forged the letter, why did the friends blame Liam? The listener does not hold present danger. Violet is already dead. The four are described retrospectively. Brad’s disappearance and Margo’s approaching death are absent. There is no closing window, threatened person or immediate consequence inside the stated minute.

**Severity:** Fatal under the standing cold-open and jeopardy gates.

**What would have to be true:** Blind listeners would need to experience an immediate threat rather than merely understand the premise, and the minute would need to make a live consequence or closing window legible without later context. Only an actual blind read can establish the first requirement. The text does not establish the second.

## 6. The new facts

### Brad reads the debt book on Day 0

**Earliest competent action:** Brad goes to the boatshed immediately after the murder, cuts or defeats the new padlock and destroys whatever Violet stored there.

“The only keys are with the solicitor” prevents legitimate unlocking. It does not make a padlock physically invulnerable. Brad knows an unknown letter and incriminating object are waiting behind it. Leaving both untouched for fourteen days, then risking interception at the post office and being seen entering at dawn, is not his least-cost action. An empty store and a damaged lock are safer for him than evidence he has not read.

**Severity:** Fatal.

**What would have to be true:** Physical entry before Day 14 would have to be impossible or demonstrably more incriminating than allowing four witnesses to open and read unknown evidence. The delta establishes neither.

### The ashes and the sister who owns the house

**Earliest competent action:** The sister notices the family remains missing when she gains access on Day 5 and asks the four or the police where they are.

This is not a misplaced ornament. It is her mother’s cremated remains, removed from a known shelf immediately before or after Violet’s death. Before Day 14, none of the four can give the proposed explanation that Violet asked them to keep the tube. Their ignorance confirms that the remains are missing. That supplies concrete evidence of entry or removal before Brad has completed the substitution.

The sister’s assumption that Violet “finally took her out to the point” is unsupported. Violet had rejected both boat offers, left no scattering arrangement and died before explaining any change of mind. A family member clearing the house cannot competently convert missing human remains into a settled assumption.

**Severity:** Fatal.

**What would have to be true:** The sister would need not to know where the ashes were kept, not to inventory the room, and to possess independent reason to believe Violet had already scattered them before dying.

### Liam’s two boat requests

**Earliest competent action:** Liam explains them on Monday night, as soon as Ruby reads the starter list.

He does not need until Meeting 3 to remember why he offered. Four years had passed, and he thought an offer would spare Violet from initiating the decision. That explanation is immediate, consistent with his conduct and readily understood by people who knew Violet. His not asking why after she refused makes the conduct less suspicious.

**Severity:** Serious, and fatal to the Liam decoy as presently constructed.

**What would have to be true:** Liam’s explanation would have to be unavailable, contradicted by an independent fact or connected to possession of Violet’s key. None applies.

### The forged police ban

**Earliest competent action:** Ruby takes both letters and the account of possible unauthorized entry to Del on Day 14 or Day 15.

A dead friend’s request cannot prohibit Ruby from reporting a possible intrusion connected to that friend’s death. The phrase “not the police” is itself conspicuous enough to provoke scrutiny. Del now has a genuine handwritten note and the locker letter, an obvious reason to preserve the original, compare the documents, identify key holders, speak to the solicitor and investigate access to the boatsheds. That ends the private three-week kitchen-table process.

The claim that police have “nothing to act on” is false. It may not be enough to prove murder, but it is enough to record a suspected intrusion, preserve documents and interview the recipients. A competent inquiry into boatshed access creates the possibility of finding the dawn witness immediately. The delta merely declares that the sighting remains hidden.

**Severity:** Fatal.

**What would have to be true:** Police procedure would have to leave the original letter with the group, perform no document comparison, conduct no key inquiry and make no attempt to establish who accessed the store. That institutional passivity would need specific support.

### “The ninth”

**Earliest competent action:** On Monday night, the four ask where each person was on the ninth and who could have entered Violet’s house that day.

The exact date narrows the inquiry instead of salting it. Liam’s legitimate bedroom visit occurred in spring, not on the ninth. Unless he retained a key, the date separates him from the triggering event. Work records, boat movements, phone locations, neighbours and the sister’s movements may eliminate members of the group or identify outside access. At minimum, it reinforces Violet’s statement that none of them was responsible.

The delta says the ninth falls after the last visitor and before the store letter. That does not neutralise the date. It makes an unauthorized entry on a defined day more significant and more investigable.

**Severity:** Serious, and potentially fatal to the inward turn.

**What would have to be true:** No usable records or witnesses could exist, nobody could establish an alibi, and Liam would need some independent connection to the house on that date. The delta provides none.

**REJECT. Confidence: high. The decisive finding is Brad’s Day 0 least-cost action. Once he knows that an unknown letter and object capable of exposing him are protected only by a new padlock, waiting fourteen days to read them is irrational. He can defeat the padlock and destroy the contents before the four receive their keys. The entire premise depends on the killer voluntarily preserving the mechanism that can expose him.**
