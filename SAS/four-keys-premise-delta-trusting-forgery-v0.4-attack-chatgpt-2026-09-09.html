  1. Brad’s fourteen days

Objection: Section 4 protects the shackle, not the locker. The day is Day 1, at first light. Brad opens his own locker beside Violet’s, giving himself both a legitimate reason to be present and a visual screen. He then attacks the enclosure rather than the padlock: shared partition, roof, rear panel, hinge pins, hasp fasteners or an existing gap sufficient for a camera. Anything removable can be replaced before he leaves. Nothing need look wrong from the aisle.

This is better than waiting. He learns what the letter contains thirteen days earlier, gains time to compose the forgery without a fourteen-hour deadline, and can decide whether substitution is even necessary. The boat owner sees only an ordinary visit to Brad’s own locker.

The closed-shackle answer fails because it rules out shimming, cutting and unskilled picking, then silently treats those as the only means of entry. It never describes the locker itself.

Severity: Serious. The conclusion that Brad waits may still be true, but the least-cost argument has not earned it.

What would have to be true for this to be answered: Violet’s locker must be a self-contained metal enclosure with inaccessible or non-removable hinges, protected fasteners, no shared partition, no exploitable roof or rear access, no useful gaps, and no way Brad could inspect it from his adjacent locker without leaving detectable damage. Brad must know those facts before deciding to wait.

  1. The room

Objection: Liam says five people knew about the meter-box key. The unanswered line is:

“Five people that we know about. If Vi knew it wasn’t us, why aren’t we looking for the person she thought knew?”

The room cannot know that Violet never told anyone else, was never observed retrieving the key, never gave a tradesperson access, and never allowed somebody to copy it. Her own letter states both that the intruder had a key and that the intruder was not one of the four. The meter box therefore supports the existence of an uncounted person at least as strongly as it contradicts Violet’s clearance.

Ruby’s later line, “she thought she’d know, and she’s dead,” converts the death into proof that Violet misjudged her friends. It is not proof. Violet could have misjudged an outsider, the safety of the hiding place, or whether anybody had observed it. The spare being returned exactly where it belonged distinguishes nobody.

Severity: Fatal. The premise depends on competent people constructing the accusation themselves. At present they close the suspect pool by assertion and then mistake the closed pool for evidence.

What would have to be true for this to be answered: The four must possess reliable grounds for knowing that access knowledge was confined to them, not merely that they were the only people Liam remembers being told. Violet’s written clearance must also be weaker than that independent knowledge. Neither condition exists in the delta.

  1. Margo

Objection: Margo’s earliest competent action occurs the moment her probate reconciliation shows that Brad paid nothing. She tells the executor that Violet’s estate may hold a disputed receivable, supplies her dated file note naming Brad and the sum, and records the item for investigation rather than writing “no receivables evidenced.”

A contemporaneous accountant’s note is evidence. It may not prove an enforceable debt by itself, but “not conclusive” is not “not evidenced.” Violet’s refusal to execute loan papers does not erase Margo’s record, the original transfer or withdrawal trail, Violet’s later calling-in, or Brad’s capacity to admit or dispute the account. The executor can demand confirmation from him. Whatever Brad answers becomes a surviving record.

The client-confidence answer fails at exactly this point. Margo has been engaged by Violet’s executor to close Violet’s books. She is not gossiping to the room or volunteering information to police. She is reporting a potential estate asset to the legal representative responsible for administering that estate. Violet’s choice not to document the loan is not an instruction to conceal it after death, especially after Violet began calling it in.

There is no reason in the delta for the sister not to chase it. She does not chase it because Margo falsely converts “insufficient proof” into “nothing to report.” That is not Violet’s omission. It is Margo’s.

Severity: Fatal. The probate-arrival repair destroys the silence it was introduced to justify. Once Margo performs the work competently, Brad’s debt enters an executor’s records, Margo’s suspicion is no longer private, and Brad cannot kill merely to extinguish what he reads in her silence.

What would have to be true for this to be answered: Margo’s note must contain too little to identify Brad, the transaction or any recoverable amount; the underlying financial records must provide no corroboration; and Margo must have a defensible professional basis for withholding even the existence of the disputed claim from the executor. The current text states the opposite on the first point and supplies nothing on the others.

  1. Chapter 7

Objection: The box number turns Brad into a liar who secretly entered the locker before the agreed meeting. It does not turn him into the forger.

The postmark establishes when the envelope travelled. The box number establishes Brad’s earlier opportunity to collect it. The sighting establishes that he used the opportunity. None establishes what he found, what he removed, or whether the letter read at nine differed from the letter Violet deposited.

Ally must still assume that Brad did more than read early. She must assume he removed an authentic letter, learned its contents, forged a replacement, returned it, and later presented that replacement. There is no surviving authentic text against which to establish substitution.

The omissions do not bridge the gap. Knowing that Violet carried the panel around town does not prove she mentioned it in this particular letter. Knowing that she was calling in money does not prove she named the debt. “Kind to exactly one man” and “did not sound like Vi” remain readings of the disputed document. They acquire suspicion from Brad’s access, but they do not prove alteration.

The claim that “one man is named without a word about handwriting” overstates the evidence. One man is named as the only person known to have disobeyed Violet’s instruction and accessed the locker early. The decisive act remains assumed.

Severity: Serious. It is a valid suspect turn. It is not a forgery turn, and it cannot carry a fair-play solution if presented as one.

What would have to be true for this to be answered: Chapter 7 must require only the conclusion that Brad deserves investigation, not that he forged the letter. If it claims the latter, there must be independent evidence that the deposited text and the nine o’clock text were different.

  1. Violet

Objection: The earliest competent challenge comes from the first signwriter who tells Violet the panel is hers:

“Have you shown Brad? He copied your alphabet for seven years. Why are you asking me before you ask him?”

Her answer, “I never taught him leaf,” does not survive the next sentence:

“That means you didn’t teach him. It doesn’t mean he never learned.”

The teacher-pride repair fails because Violet has already carried the panel around the trade and confessed to professional peers that she cannot account for a piece apparently made in her own hand. She is willing to expose the uncertainty to café staff, cinema staff and competing signwriters. Her pride becomes selectively paralysing only when she reaches the person most qualified to identify the alphabet.

It also makes “the best judge of a hand on the coast” reason badly. Gilding is a technique. The design, spacing, stroke decisions and maker’s mark are attribution evidence. Brad’s seven years under Violet make him the obvious comparator whether he laid the leaf himself, learned elsewhere or had the leaf applied by somebody else. “He never asked me” is not knowledge that he cannot do it.

Severity: Serious. Violet can refuse to show him, but the story has not prevented the obvious question from being asked or made her professional exclusion competent.

What would have to be true for this to be answered: The people Violet consults must not know that Brad trained under her, and Violet’s expertise must give her a sound basis for excluding his underlying hand from the finished panel solely because she did not teach him one production technique. The delta establishes neither.

  1. The new and changed facts

The closed-shackle padlock

The earliest competent action is Brad’s Day 1 inspection of the whole enclosure from his adjacent locker. If any partition, rear panel, roof surface, hinge or fastening can be removed and restored, he reads the letter without touching the protected shackle.

Severity: Serious.

What would have to be true: Every non-lock access route must be physically excluded and known by Brad to be excluded.

The sister’s belief that the ashes were scattered

The earliest competent action is on Day 14, when the four discover that the tube contains the remains of the executor’s mother. One of them tells the sister. They do not wait for her to ask about remains she believes do not exist.

The sister then demands the tube, asks why Violet lied about the scattering, and involves the solicitor if the four claim Violet instructed them to retain it. The ashes no longer sit unreported on Ruby’s sideboard for six weeks. The supposedly closed room gains an executor with a direct family interest and knowledge of Violet’s unexplained deception.

Section 8 answers why the sister does not ask before Day 14. It does not answer why four competent adults conceal the discovery from her after Day 14.

Severity: Serious.

What would have to be true: All four must have a credible reason to believe Violet’s posthumous instruction overrides any obligation to notify the executor and surviving daughter of the person whose remains they are holding.

“No receivables evidenced”

The earliest competent action begins when Margo undertakes the probate work, no later than Day 47. She reports the named, quantified and recently called-in loan as a possible or disputed estate asset. The executor sends Brad a demand or request for confirmation. Brad’s response and Margo’s supporting note survive her.

Severity: Fatal.

What would have to be true: Margo’s dated note must not amount even to evidence requiring disclosure or inquiry, and her engagement must somehow forbid her from telling the executor about it. The text gives her the evidence and the engagement.

The undated forgery

The earliest competent action occurs at Ruby’s table on Day 14:

“Vi dated everything. Why is the letter we’re supposed to act on undated, and what does ‘last month’ mean without a date?”

The missing date prevents alibi testing, but it also prevents the room from treating the letter as a usable chronology. Combined with the police line Ruby already identifies as foreign to Violet’s voice, it creates an authenticity problem before the lists begin. Competent adults preserve the document and withhold reliance on it until its provenance is examined. Even if later handwriting examination is inconclusive, the six-week exercise cannot honestly proceed as obedience to Violet’s uncontested instructions.

Section 8 answers investigability by deleting the date. It does not answer the behavioural cost of giving a meticulous recorder an undated instruction containing relative time.

Severity: Serious.

What would have to be true: Violet must commonly leave important written instructions undated, and the four must know that habit well enough that neither the omission nor “last month” troubles them.

The meter box as the room’s turn

The earliest competent action is Day 15, immediately after Liam says five people knew. Someone corrects him to “five people we know of” and asks who else had entered the property, serviced the meter, watched Violet retrieve the key, received temporary access or could have copied it. The suspect pool opens rather than closes.

Reporting the letter to police does not answer this. Even if the police find nothing, failure to identify an outsider does not convert the four into a complete suspect set. The core inward turn never occurs.

Severity: Fatal.

What would have to be true: The four must independently know that nobody outside their group could know or discover the hiding place. Liam’s recollection cannot establish that negative.
