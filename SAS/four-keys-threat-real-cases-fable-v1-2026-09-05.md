## Part one: the real world

A note before the cases. Working from memory only, the honest finding is that the documented record is thin on what the brief most wants: a household object rigged in advance so that the victim's own routine kills them. Most quiet domestic murders that reached a courtroom were either staged after the act (the body arranged in the bath, the car in the garage) or exploited a routine with a substance rather than a mechanism. The rigged-appliance murder lives mostly in fiction, which is exactly why constraint 8 in part two matters. What the real cases do teach, reliably, is which routines intimates choose (the bath, sleep, the daily cup, the moment under a supported weight), what the first crack in the story usually was, and how long an accident ruling can stand when everyone in the room already believes it. Where I am unsure of a detail I say so.

**1. Kenneth Barlow and Elizabeth Barlow, Bradford, England, May 1957.** Husband and wife; he was a state-enrolled nurse. He injected his wife with insulin while she was drowsy, and she lost consciousness in her evening bath and slid under the water. His account was that she had been unwell, gone to bathe, and that he fell asleep and found her submerged, pulled her out and tried artificial respiration. It relied on her nightly bath, which he of course knew, and on the fact that insulin was then thought undetectable after death. What made police suspicious was small and physical: his pyjamas were dry when he said he had hauled a drowning woman from a full bath, there was no water splashed on the floor, and water still lay in the crook of her elbow, meaning she had not been lifted. Her pupils were dilated and her pyjamas and pillow were soaked with sweat, which the pathologist read as hypoglycaemia. Two syringes were found in the kitchen; he said they were for his own penicillin. Four injection marks were found in her buttocks, and insulin was demonstrated by injecting extracts from the tissue into laboratory mice. He was convicted that summer. An ordinary observer would have read the scene as a faint in the bath; only someone who knew what a rescue leaves on a floor read it otherwise. Instructive because the lie was undone not by the method but by what the method's cover story physically required and failed to produce.

**2. George Joseph Smith, the "Brides in the Bath," England, 1912 to 1914.** Bigamist husband of Bessie Mundy (Herne Bay), Alice Burnham (Blackpool) and Margaret Lofty (Highgate). In each case he married, had a will made or insurance taken, walked the woman to a doctor days before with a complaint of headaches or fits so that a medical history existed, and then she was found drowned in a bath in rented rooms while he had gone out for an errand. At Herne Bay he bought the bath himself and, as I recall, returned it to the ironmonger afterwards. There was no tampering; the method was to seize the ankles and pull, so the head went under and the sudden flood of water into the nose and throat caused a shock that was later reproduced, nearly fatally, in an experiment on a volunteer nurse for the pathologist Spilsbury. The routine exploited was simply the bath in a strange lodging, with the husband the only other person in the house. Suspicion came from outside: the father of Alice Burnham and a Blackpool landlord each read a newspaper report of the third death and were struck by the resemblance, and wrote to the police. Inquests had already returned accidental death, with a fit in the bath as the accepted explanation, and to every doctor who attended it had looked like exactly that. Instructive because a single instance was unreadable; the crime was only visible as a pattern, and only to people who had a reason to keep a private tally.

**3. Robert "Rattlesnake" James and Mary Busch James, Los Angeles, 1935.** Husband and wife, a barber who had insured her. He and an accomplice bought two rattlesnakes, restrained his pregnant wife in the kitchen and had the snakes bite her leg; when the venom was slow, he drowned her in the bathtub and then carried her out and put her face down in the small lily pond in the garden so it would look as though she had fainted and fallen in. The first ruling was accidental drowning. It unravelled some time later, and my memory is that the accomplice talked and that James's earlier history (a previous wife had also drowned in a bathtub, in Colorado, in circumstances never proven) came into it once investigators had a reason to look; I am not certain of the exact chain. The physical evidence that mattered afterwards was the swollen, punctured leg, which the first examiners had attributed to the fall or to post-mortem change. An ordinary observer read a woman found in a shallow pond as a faint. He was hanged in 1942. Instructive because a truly bizarre mechanism was hidden inside an utterly ordinary one: nobody looked past the drowning to ask why she had drowned.

**4. Karl Karlsen, Murrieta, California, 1991, and Romulus, New York, 2008.** Husband, then father. In 1991 his wife Christina died in a house fire; she was trapped in a bathroom whose window had been boarded over (reported at the time as covering a broken pane), and he had recently insured her. It was treated as a tragic accident for nearly three decades. In 2008 his adult son Levi, twenty-three, was crushed under a pickup truck that came down off its jack while he was working under it in the family barn; Karl had taken out a large life policy on him days earlier. He pleaded guilty in 2013 to murdering Levi, having admitted the truck did not come down by itself, and in 2020 was convicted in California of murdering Christina. The routine exploited was the son's habit of working under vehicles on the property and the mechanical fact that a vehicle on a jack is held up by one thing. To anyone arriving at the scene, a truck off a jack is the commonest fatal accident in any rural county, and the first responders read it as one. What first made anyone suspicious was not physical: it was the insurance, the second wife's later statement, and the accumulation of two convenient deaths around one man. Instructive because removing a single support and letting gravity do the work leaves no tool marks, and the only evidence is who benefits.

**5. Colin Howell, Lesley Howell and Trevor Buchanan, Castlerock, Northern Ireland, May 1991.** Howell was a dentist; Lesley was his wife, Trevor was the husband of Howell's lover. Both were found dead in a car in a garage at Castlerock with a hose from the exhaust, and for eighteen years it stood as a suicide pact between two betrayed spouses. In 2009 Howell confessed to church elders and then police. He had gassed his wife at home with exhaust fumes run through a hose while she was drowsy (my memory is that she had been given something to help her sleep, but I am not certain), then gone to the Buchanan house and done the same to Trevor in his bed, then driven both bodies to the garage and arranged them. It relied on knowing when each would be asleep and alone. There was no physical evidence that pointed to murder in 1991; carbon monoxide was the cause in both, which was consistent with the staging, and the whole community read it as grief and betrayal. Instructive because carbon monoxide gives a cause of death that is true and a story that is false, and the story held for as long as nobody had a reason to distrust the one man who had written it.

**6. Kathleen Savio and Drew Peterson, Bolingbrook, Illinois, March 2004.** Ex-husband, a serving police sergeant, with intimate knowledge of the house. Savio was found dead in a dry bathtub with a gash to the back of her head and bruising; a coroner's jury ruled accidental drowning, on the picture of a woman who had slipped in the bath, struck her head and drowned, the water later draining. That ruling stood for three years. Only when Peterson's fourth wife Stacy disappeared in 2007 was Savio exhumed; a second autopsy found the injuries inconsistent with a simple fall, and the death was reclassified as homicide. Peterson was convicted in 2012. The physical evidence in 2004 was the head wound, the bruises on the body, the dry tub, and the awkwardness of the position, all of which an ordinary observer, and several professionals, read as a slip. Instructive because it shows how much a scene can carry when the person explaining it is trusted and no one in the room wants a second story; the evidence did not change between 2004 and 2007, only the willingness to read it.

**7. Ian Stewart, Helen Bailey (2016) and Diane Stewart (2010), Hertfordshire and Cambridgeshire, England.** Partner in the first case, husband in the second. Bailey, a writer, was dosed for weeks with zopiclone, a sleeping drug, in what she was eating or drinking; she complained to friends of tiredness and of feeling strange, and it was read as a bad patch. He then smothered her and hid her body, with her dog, in a cesspit beneath the garage, and told everyone she had gone away to a coastal retreat. Hair analysis later showed the drug had been given over a sustained period. Suspicion came from the disappearance itself and his behaviour around her money. His first wife Diane had died in the garden in 2010 and been recorded as a sudden death from epilepsy; after the Bailey conviction her retained brain tissue was re-examined and he was convicted of her murder in 2022. The routine exploited in the first case was her daily drinks in her own kitchen, which he shared. Nothing about the weeks of dosing was physically visible; an ordinary observer saw a tired woman. Instructive because the "wear" was the woman herself: a long, undramatic decline that every friend accepted as life.

**8. Caroline Grills, Sydney, Australia, 1947 to 1953.** Elderly relative by marriage to all her victims; she poisoned tea with thallium, then sold openly as a rat poison, killing her stepmother and three other relations over several years and blinding a fifth. The routine was the afternoon cup in the victims' own homes, which she poured because she was the kind of family visitor who always made the tea. Symptoms (hair falling out, numbness, failing sight) were read as age and illness by doctors and family. Suspicion came from within the household: a son-in-law of one surviving victim noticed her handling the cups and had a cup tested. She was convicted in 1953. There was no physical evidence at all until someone decided to keep a cup. Instructive because it is the pure case of a killer whose only tool was being welcome, and whose only exposure was one relative who stopped treating a coincidence as a coincidence.

**9. Kanae Kijima, Japan, 2007 to 2009.** She met men through marriage-agency introductions, took money from them, and three of them died of carbon monoxide poisoning from charcoal briquettes burned in a sealed room or car, each death staged as suicide; sedatives were found in the victims. In two of the deaths, as I recall, the scene was the man's own home. The routine relied on was sleep after a drink or meal she had prepared. Charcoal-briquette suicide was at that time a recognised pattern in Japan, so each death read as one more, and it was only when investigators linked the men through money transfers to the same woman that the pattern was seen. She was sentenced to death in 2012 and the sentence was upheld. Instructive because she chose a method the culture already had a story for; the staging did not have to be good, only familiar.

**10. Claus and Sunny von Bülow, Newport, Rhode Island, December 1979 and December 1980.** Husband and wife. Sunny fell into a coma at home twice, in consecutive Decembers, with profound hypoglycaemia; she recovered from the first and never from the second. Her children from her first marriage hired a private investigator, and the family maid and stepson found a black bag in a closet containing drugs and a needle encrusted with insulin. Von Bülow was convicted in 1982 and acquitted at retrial in 1985, with the defence arguing that the encrusted needle was inconsistent with injection (an injected needle wipes clean on withdrawal), that Sunny had a history of self-medication and low blood sugar, and that the bag's chain of custody was hopeless. I include it not for the verdict but because it is the documented case of an object whose meaning could not be settled: the same needle read as proof of murder to one jury and as a plant or an irrelevance to another. Instructive because an ambiguous physical detail found by an interested party is worth almost nothing in court and almost everything to the person who found it.

One more, briefly, for the record. **Craig Rabinowitz, Merion, Pennsylvania, April 1997**: strangled his wife Stefanie and put her in the bath so it would read as a drowning; the medical examiner found signs of strangulation and a sedative in her system, and his debts and double life surfaced within days. He pleaded guilty. The lesson repeats Barlow: the bath is the intimate's favourite room because it explains a wet, undressed, unconscious body without a word.

What these have in common that part two can use: the killer never invents a hazard, he borrows one the house already has; the window is the daily moment of nakedness, sleep or trust; the first ruling is almost always accident or natural causes; and the crack, when it comes, is a small physical thing that contradicts the cover story (a dry floor, a dry tub, a punctured leg) or a pattern kept by someone who had a private reason to count.

## Part two: what the real world teaches, applied

Shared assumptions for every candidate. Violet's spare key lives under the third loose brick of her back yard wall, and every one of the four has used it at her invitation at some point; Liam also holds a cut key. Any of the four could therefore be inside the house while she is in the water, which she is for about fifty minutes every morning at a time known to the minute. Nobody else in the city has that key knowledge, and the house is locked when she swims. The tampering in each case is inside; the yard brick is only the door.

**Candidate 1: the signboard over the standing place.**
It descends from Karl Karlsen and the truck on the jack: remove the one thing holding a weight up and let the victim's habit put them under it. Violet eats standing up, always at the same stretch of kitchen counter by the window, under the first sign she ever made, a solid oak board with cast letters that she hung there twenty years ago on two brass mirror plates. One morning, back from the slip and eating toast, she turns to the kettle and the board drops behind her, its lower edge splitting the counter top where her head was a second earlier. The habit is the standing place; only people who have eaten in that kitchen know she never sits, and that she stands exactly there because it is the spot with the sea in the window. When she looks, both mirror plates are still screwed to the board, but the two wall screws are gone entirely, not sheared or pulled through, and the wall plugs are clean and intact with no plaster dust below them; then she finds the screws themselves standing in the pen pot on the dresser, heads still ringed with the cream paint of her own kitchen, where someone put them down and forgot them. To an officer, an old board came off an old wall; to a joiner, "they were never fixed into anything, love, it was always going to come away," and screws in a pot are screws in a pot. She photographs the clean plugs with a ruler beside them and keeps the two painted screws in an envelope. The thing that would make it fail as story is the doubt about whether a falling board kills a fit woman rather than concussing her; it needs the weight and the edge stated once, and a reader who has seen a headstone fall.

**Candidate 2: the soot door on the stove flue.**
It descends from Colin Howell and Kanae Kijima, the sleeper and the gas, with Savio's three-year accident ruling behind it. Violet lights the wood stove in the back room every evening from October and banks it down to slumber overnight, which is when a stove makes the most carbon monoxide and the least heat. She wakes at four with a headache like a blow, is sick on the landing floor, and gets the sash up before she understands anything; in daylight she goes down and finds the little steel soot door on the flue pipe, an inspection plate the size of a playing card held by two screws, lying face down on the hearth with its screws gone, so that the slumbering stove had been breathing straight into the room and up the house all night. The habit is the overnight bank, a thing only people who have sat late in that room and watched her do it know, because from the street the house is dark and the chimney barely smokes. What says deliberate to her is that the plate is face down, sooty side up, laid flat rather than fallen, and that the two machine screws are nowhere, not on the hearth, not in the ash pan; a plate that vibrates off leaves its screws or their stubs. Her carbon monoxide alarm lives in the kitchen, where she once thought the danger was, and never sounded. To an officer, the plate came loose, these things do; a chimney sweep says the same and points to the soft threads in the old pipe. She photographs the plate where it lies before touching it, and keeps it. The thing that would make it fail is that a blocked or leaking flue is close to the television standard, and readers with a stove will ask why the room did not stink of smoke, which needs the one honest answer, that a slumbering stove smells of nothing at all.

**Candidate 3: the water heater over the bath.**
It descends from Barlow, Smith and Savio, the bath as the killing room, with Howell's gas as the agent. Violet comes in from the harbour blue with cold and gets straight into a hot bath, door shut, towel along the bottom to keep the draught out, with the old gas multipoint heater on the wall above the taps thundering away; it is a small room. On a still morning in winter she finds herself on the bathroom floor with the door open and no memory of opening it, and a headache she puts down to the cold water until she sees the heater's flame is yellow and lifting. Behind the casing she finds the flue collar has been lifted off its spigot and set back down a quarter turn out, its seam facing the wall, and the three self-tappers that fix it are missing, with bright unrusted marks where their heads sat. The habit is the shut door and the towel, a swimmer's habit, and the timing; only the four have been in the house at seven in the morning and heard the heater roar behind that door. What says deliberate is that a collar which slips falls down, it does not rotate and settle, and that three screws do not leave together. To a gas fitter it is a condemned appliance and he is amazed she is alive, and to an officer it is an old heater in an old house and a warning to get a modern one. She photographs the collar seam and the three bright marks and keeps the towel, absurdly, because she cannot think what else to keep. The thing that would make it fail is that it is a second carbon monoxide scenario and the world's most obvious "faulty boiler," and a reader who has watched a Sunday drama will feel they have seen it.

**Candidate 4: the earth and the live in the shower isolator.**
It descends from the bath-window pattern of Barlow, Smith, Savio and Rabinowitz, the one daily moment of being wet, naked and alone; I have no documented parent for the mechanism itself and say so. Violet does not use the upstairs bath in summer; she runs into the cold little back scullery and stands under the electric shower fitted there for exactly the post-swim rinse, on a stone floor, with the door open to the yard. One morning she reaches for the hose and the shock throws her against the wall; the old fuse board has no trip on that circuit, which is why she is bruised and not dead, because she let go. In the pull-cord isolator on the ceiling she finds the earth conductor and the live have been swapped at the terminals, and, on the floor of the airing cupboard where the cable runs, the little green and yellow sleeve that should be on the earth core, lying loose, with the copper it came off freshly stripped and bright. The habit is that she uses the scullery shower and not the bathroom, and only people who have seen her come dripping through the kitchen in June know it. What says deliberate is the sleeve on the floor and the bright copper: a transposition done years ago by a bad electrician would have dull copper and no loose sleeve, and the shower has never bitten her in nine years. To an electrician the installation is a disgrace and someone wired it wrong once; to an officer it is old wiring and she was lucky. She photographs the terminals before he touches them and keeps the sleeve. The thing that would make it fail is that the electrified shower is only a step from the hair dryer in the bath, and a reader who knows houses will ask why there was no trip, which the age of the house answers but only just.

**Candidate 5: thinners in the paraffin.**
It descends from Karlsen's 1991 fire, and from Grills in a different key: the domestic vessel refilled with something that looks the same. Violet heats the back room, where she draws up her lettering at night, with an old paraffin heater she fills every Sunday from the can under the sink. She unscrews the can on Sunday evening, tilts it, and stops, because the smell coming up is cellulose thinners from her own trade, not paraffin, and a wick heater lit on thinners in a closed room goes up in a sheet. The habit is Sunday filling from that can; only people who have sat at that table on a winter Sunday have watched her do it, and only people who know her trade know she keeps thinners at the unit and never in the house. When she looks she finds the paraffin can's cap has a new retail tamper ring, intact, on a can she opened herself a month ago, and the label, in her own hand, has a fresh thumbprint in thinners that has lifted the ink; she ranks handwriting for a living and she knows her own label had no smudge. What says deliberate is the ring: cans do not reseal themselves. To an officer she is a signwriter with two cans of solvent under her sink who has mixed them up, and a fire officer would say gently that this is how half the house fires in the city start. She keeps the can, cap and all, and photographs the ring. The thing that would make it fail is that it never happens, she only nearly lights it, so the scene has to carry its own dread without a flame, and a reader may feel that a woman who smelled it first was never in danger.

**Candidate 6: the sash cords in the back bedroom.**
It descends from Karlsen again, the removed support, and from Smith's choice of the one repeated moment. Every morning before she goes down, Violet throws up the heavy back sash and leans out over the yards to read the sea. One morning the sash comes down as she pulls her head in and takes a strip of skin off her shoulder and a hank of hair; a second slower and it would have had her across the back of the neck with the whole weight of Victorian glass and timber. In the pocket of the box frame she finds both cords cut square through, not frayed at the pulley where they always go, and the cut ends tucked up into the pocket so the sash would hold on paint and friction until it was pushed to its full height, as she pushes it every day. The habit is the lean, and the full throw of the sash; the tampering only works on someone who opens it right to the top and puts her body through it, and that is a thing only people who have stayed in the house have seen, because the window faces the yards. What says deliberate is the square cut and the tucked ends: rotten cord breaks ragged and drops the weight to the bottom of the box with a bang she would have heard. To an officer and to a glazier, sash cords are the commonest failure in the street and everyone in Mariner's Row has had one go. She photographs the cut ends and keeps a length of cord. The thing that would make it fail is that the lean is visible from a dozen back windows, so it points at intimates less cleanly than the others, and a reader will say so.

**Candidate 7: the pressure cooker's safety.**
It descends from Karlsen, the defeated safety and the ordinary use that then kills. Violet cooks one big pan of beans every Sunday in her mother's old pressure cooker, standing over it, and the four have all eaten from it. This Sunday the weight does not start to rock when it should and she, being the kind of person who notices, lifts the pan off the ring and lets it cool rather than waiting. When she looks she finds the vent tube plugged solid with a plug of solder and, where the little fusible safety plug should be in the lid, a plain steel bolt of the right thread, sealed with a smear of blue thread lock she does not own. The habit is Sunday, the beans, and standing over it with her face above the lid; only people who have eaten in that kitchen know the pan or the day. What says deliberate is that both escapes are closed, not one; a pan that fails by wear loses a seal, it does not gain a bolt. To an officer she has a badly repaired pan, and someone has bodged it, probably years ago, probably her; to any repair man it is a bodge he has seen a hundred times. She keeps the lid and photographs the bolt with the thread lock still wet. The thing that would make it fail is that the reader has to be told a pressure cooker can kill, and the telling is a paragraph, which is exactly what constraint 7 forbids.

**Candidate 8: the foil in the electric blanket's fuse.**
It descends from Karlsen's house fire and from Stewart's slow use of a nightly routine. Violet is a cold-water swimmer who puts her electric blanket on after every evening swim and falls asleep on it more nights than not, and complains to all four about her feet. One night she wakes to a smell of hot dust and finds the blanket scorched brown in a line where the element has folded under the mattress and pinched; she pulls it out and stands on the landing shaking. In the morning she opens the blanket's controller plug and finds the fuse holder carrying no fuse but a rolled sleeve of kitchen foil, the oldest bodge there is, and she has never bodged a plug in her life; a fused plug would have blown and gone cold at the first fault. The habit is the blanket left on under a sleeping woman, which only people who have listened to her complain about her feet know about, and the fold is on the side she sleeps on. What says deliberate is the foil, present where a fuse should be, in a plug she bought sealed. To an officer it is foil in a fuse and everyone has done it; to an electrician it is proof she is careless. She keeps the plug and photographs the scorch line against the mattress. The thing that would make it fail is that the reader has to accept that the foil was put there and the fold made at the same time, which is one act but looks like two, and the scene risks reading as a warning rather than an attempt.

**Forced ranking, best first.**

1. **The signboard** beats the sash because the killing spot is only knowable from inside the kitchen, the deliberate detail (two painted screws in a pen pot, clean plugs) is the purest "placed not fallen" of the set, and nobody has seen it on television.
2. **The sash cords** beat the soot door because a cut cord tucked into a box frame is a cleaner physical fact than a plate that might have vibrated off, and it is not a gas story; it loses to the signboard only because the lean can be seen from the yards.
3. **The soot door** beats thinners in the paraffin because it actually happens to her, she wakes poisoned, and the overnight slumbering stove is a real killer that a reader accepts in one line; it loses to the sash because "faulty flue" is halfway to the standard drama.
4. **Thinners in the paraffin** beats the water heater because it uses her trade and her eye for her own handwriting, and the retail ring on a can she opened is unarguable to her and nothing to anyone else; it loses to the soot door because nothing happens.
5. **The water heater over the bath** beats the shower isolator because its habit link to the swim is the strongest of all eight and the rotated collar is a good detail; it loses to the paraffin because it is the second carbon monoxide story and the most familiar.
6. **The shower isolator** beats the electric blanket because the loose earth sleeve and bright copper are a sharper found detail than foil, and a shock survived is a better scene than a scorch; it loses to the heater because it stands too close to the hair dryer in the bath.
7. **The electric blanket** beats the pressure cooker because a reader believes a bed fire without being taught anything; it loses to the shower because the foil-and-fold looks like two acts, and the scene reads as a warning.
8. **The pressure cooker** is last because it fails constraint 7 outright: nobody believes the death without a paragraph, and the ranking does not need to say more than that.
