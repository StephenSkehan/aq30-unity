1.

About six weeks before the end of the first act, Violet raises her post-swim flask to her mouth at the unit and smells brush restorer beneath the tea. She has not swallowed any, but the smell comes from the absorbent lining of the flask cap rather than the drink itself.

Only the four know she fills that flask before dawn, drinks directly through its cap after swimming, and stores it in a locked cupboard at the unit. Brad has the regular key, while the concealed spare behind the fixed radio has been shown only to Liam, Ruby, Margo and Brad.

The masking-tape label on the cap appears to be hers, but Violet sees pauses in the curved letters where somebody traced rather than wrote them. Beneath it is the missing label from a brush-restorer container, and the two torn edges match.

The police would see ordinary workplace contamination involving a signwriter who stores solvents nearby. An officer could photograph it, but without fingerprints, a witness or proof of who handled the cap, there is no identifiable offence or suspect.

She could seal and keep the cap, both layers of tape and the matching torn label from the solvent container.

It fails if the chemical smell is so strong that no rational killer could expect her to drink from the flask.

2.

Before a dawn swim, Violet checks both her bedside clock and the kitchen clock, sees the expected time and leaves her phone behind. At the slip she hears the harbour workboat signal twenty-three minutes earlier than it should be and steps back just before a trawler enters the narrow water she normally crosses.

Only the four know she swims that exact line, leaves her phone at home and trusts those two clocks rather than checking another time source. Each also knows where Liam keeps Violet's spare key when she needs someone to fetch it.

Back home, she finds that the battery-operated bedroom clock and the mains-powered kitchen clock are both precisely twenty-three minutes fast. She had synchronised them to the radio the previous evening, and the two clocks cannot acquire the same forward error through a power interruption.

A police officer would say that mis-set clocks are not evidence of attempted murder. Even accepting Violet's account, there is no proof anyone entered, no damage and no way to establish why either clock was changed.

She could photograph both clocks beside the correct time and preserve their settings without touching them.

It fails if the dangerous boat movement is not rigid and predictable enough to make the altered time look like a killing attempt rather than a cruel prank.

3.

Violet boils water on her old gas hob before swimming, turns the burner to its familiar off position and leaves. When she returns, the automatic porch light is dark, but she smells gas before entering or touching the kitchen switch and shuts the supply off from outside.

Only the four know she uses that burner before every swim, checks its position against a painted mark and returns while the kitchen is still dark. They also know that if the porch light fails, she reaches through the doorway for the kitchen switch.

Under the removable control, Violet finds its stopping point displaced from her painted mark, with a fresh crescent through the old grime. The porch bulb has also been backed out just far enough to remain dark, although she tightened it the previous day.

A police officer would see an ageing gas control and a loose bulb, two common household faults with no demonstrable connection. The officer might arrange a safety inspection, but could not identify an intruder, an offence or an intended victim.

Violet could photograph both positions before repair and keep the removed control and bulb.

It fails if the audience cannot believe the planner would rely on Violet failing to smell the gas before operating a switch.

4.

After a cold swim, Violet turns on the bathroom's old vented heater and closes the door while changing. The room behaves strangely enough that she switches the heater off immediately, opens the window and leaves without injury.

Only the four know she uses that heater solely after swims, closes the bathroom door and normally remains there while the room warms. They also know where Liam's spare key is kept.

Behind the ventilation grille, Violet finds a fitted sheet of clear plastic blocking the airflow, with the grille screws carefully replaced. The nearby carbon-monoxide alarm contains a battery, but its contact is separated by a transparent pull-tab that was not there when she tested it days earlier.

A police officer would call the plastic misguided draught-proofing and the battery tab an installation mistake. Without a harmful reading, identifiable installer or evidence of when either change occurred, the officer could not turn the combination into a provable crime.

She could keep the plastic and pull-tab and photograph the grille, screw positions and alarm before disturbing them.

It fails if the paired alterations appear so plainly coordinated that a competent officer could not reasonably dismiss them.

5.

Before preparing the Sunday meal she always cooks in an old pressure cooker, Violet performs her customary inspection and notices that its vent does not move freely. She stops before heating it and finds an obstruction where none was present when she cleaned and stored it.

Only the four know she cooks the same meal at the same time each Sunday and that she checks the cooker by touch rather than dismantling it. They alone know where the locked pantry key is hidden.

Violet removes a compact plug of dried food residue from the vent. Its outer end has been flattened and trimmed to sit flush, which tells her it was placed there rather than left by cooking.

A police officer would see an inadequately cleaned old cooker and a cautious owner who caught the problem before anything happened. The shaped residue suggests nothing provable about who placed it or whether harm was intended.

She could keep the obstruction in a small envelope and photograph it in place before removal.

It fails if the cooker's independent safety features would make the obstruction incapable of causing serious harm.

6.

After swimming, Violet switches on the kettle and goes upstairs to remove her wet clothes, exactly as she does every morning. The expected click never comes, and she returns to an acrid smell and an overheated kettle, which she unplugs before anything catches fire.

Only the four know she leaves the kitchen during that brief interval and that the kettle sits beneath a wooden cupboard. They also know how the house spare can be obtained.

A narrow folded scrap has been pressed beneath the kettle switch so it remains held down. Violet matches its torn edge to the paper lining inside her hidden cookie tin, where the money itself remains untouched.

A police officer would see a failing kettle and a piece of household paper caught in its mechanism. The matching tear might prove where the scrap originated, but not who moved it, when it arrived there or whether a fire was intended.

She could keep the scrap and the matching liner, along with photographs of the switch and the scorching.

It fails if the kettle's secondary thermal protection makes a dangerous fire impossible.

7.

Violet wakes before dawn to find her bedroom window standing open farther than the narrow gap she always leaves for air. Nothing has been stolen, including the cash in the cookie tin, but the curtain has been tucked aside so the bed can be reached directly from the window.

Only the four know which bedroom she uses, the exact width at which she fixes the window and that untouched cash would rule out an ordinary burglar in her mind. The window cannot normally open farther without releasing its restrictor.

One restrictor screw has been replaced by a shorter painted screw that grips until the window is pulled firmly. Violet finds the original screw beneath the cookie tin, a place no stranger would search without taking the money.

A police officer would say the short screw could be an old repair, the original could have been placed in the tin years ago, and wind could have moved the window. There is no proof anyone entered or intended violence.

She could keep both screws and photograph the window, curtain and untouched cash exactly as she found them.

It fails if the event reads primarily as stalking or intimidation and does not justify Violet's specific belief that death is being prepared.

8.

On a Tuesday night, Violet isolates the circuit for an illuminated sign before opening its casing, then follows her strict habit of testing it anyway. Her tester shows that the sign is still live, so she stops without touching the internal components.

Only the four know which circuit she isolates, that she performs this job alone after the market closes and where the unit's concealed spare key is kept. Nobody else knows her handwritten labelling system well enough to imitate it.

Two circuit labels have exchanged positions. The writing resembles Violet's, but she sees hesitant joins and doubled strokes made by somebody copying her lettering slowly.

A police officer would call it a dangerous labelling error in a rented commercial unit. Since Violet tested before working and cannot prove who moved the labels, it remains a workplace defect rather than a demonstrable attempt on her life.

She could remove and preserve both forged labels after photographing their positions and the live reading.

It fails because anyone who knows Violet well enough to design the trap should also know that she never trusts a label without testing the circuit.

9.

Violet returns from a swim, hangs her wet robe on its familiar bathroom peg, switches on the heater and walks to the kitchen. She smells singed cloth almost immediately and returns before the robe ignites.

Only the four know that she always hangs the robe first, starts the heater second and then leaves the room to make tea. They are the only people who would recognise the peg's position as part of that sequence.

The peg has been unscrewed and moved closer to the heater, while its original holes have been filled with Violet's own signwriter's putty. Fresh turning marks remain inside the screw head.

A police officer would see a badly positioned clothes peg beside a heater and a minor domestic near-fire. The putty and screw marks cannot establish who moved it, when they did so or whether they meant to harm Violet.

She could photograph the two peg positions and remove the screw and plug of fresh putty.

It fails if the likely consequence feels limited to a scorched robe rather than Violet's death.

10.

At the harbour slip, Violet clips on the bright tow float that makes her visible to boats and gives the buckle its habitual testing pull. The buckle opens in her hand, so she abandons the swim without entering the water.

Only the four know her precise route through the working harbour, that she swims before full daylight and that the float is her only conspicuous marker. They alone know the spare key location for the unit where she stores the swimming gear.

The buckle's retaining tongue is present but no longer catches. Under magnification, Violet sees a narrow polished patch exactly where material would need to be removed, although it could also resemble years of salt wear.

A police officer would describe it as failure of cheap plastic exposed to salt water. The officer could not prove alteration, access, intention or even that the float's loss would have placed Violet in immediate danger.

She could keep the complete buckle and photograph the polished surface before anyone handles it.

It fails if sabotaging a safety float does not feel capable of killing a strong, experienced harbour swimmer.
