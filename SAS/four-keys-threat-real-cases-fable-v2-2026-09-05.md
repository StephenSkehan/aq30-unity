**The honest gap.** Documented cases of an intimate sabotaging a victim's own house so that death would read as wear, fault or accident are rare in the record I can vouch for. What the record is full of is the neighbouring thing: staging after the act (a body arranged in a bath, a fire lit under a chair), poison slipped into a fixed routine, and devices that were never meant to pass as accidents. I know the category of the rigged shower, the wired bath and the disconnected flue exists in modern reporting (Brazil, the United States, Britain), but I cannot put a verified name and date to a single instance from memory, so none appears below as a case. The gap itself is instructive for the story: a mechanism that reads as wear does not get recorded as a crime, so it does not get a name, which is exactly the position Violet is in.

## Part one: the real world

### 1. Graham Backhouse, Horton, Gloucestershire, 1984

A farmer in serious debt; the victim was his wife Margaret. In April 1984 she got into the family Volvo in the farmyard and a pipe bomb rigged to go off when the car was used exploded under the driver's side. She survived with severe injuries to her legs and body. He had, in the preceding weeks, staged a campaign against himself: a sheep's head on a stake, a note reading "You next", threatening letters. He had raised her life insurance shortly before (the figure of £100,000 is what I recall; UNVERIFIED). The routine it relied on was that she used that car on certain mornings, which any husband knows. Police read the first event exactly as he had scripted it: an outside enemy, with Backhouse the intended target; they gave him protection. It came apart when, three weeks later, he shot his neighbour Colyn Bedale-Taylor dead with a shotgun and claimed self-defence against a man with a Stanley knife. The scene did not fit: the knife sat too neatly in the dead man's hand, the blood pattern did not match a struggle, Backhouse's own cuts were consistent with self-infliction, and the threatening note was traced to paper from the farm (the exact forensic chain is from memory; UNVERIFIED in its details). Convicted 1985, died in prison 1994. The physical evidence of the bomb itself was never ambiguous; what was ambiguous was who had placed it, and the first police reading was wrong because he had pre-loaded the story. Not inside the house, and a bomb is never wear, but it is the clearest case I know of a spouse rigging a routine object and of the victim surviving it. Instructive because it shows that the tamperer's real weapon is the story he tells beforehand about who else might want her dead, and that overreach on the second act, not the first, is what breaks it.

### 2. Jean-Pierre Vaquier, Byfleet, Surrey, 1924

A Frenchman lodging at the Blue Anchor Hotel and conducting an affair with the landlady, Mabel Jones. The victim was her husband, Alfred Poynter Jones, who took a dose of bromo salts every morning from a bottle kept in the bar parlour as a hangover cure. Vaquier put strychnine into the bottle. Jones took his usual dose on 29 March 1924 and died within the hour. The routine was public in the household and Vaquier had watched it for weeks. Suspicion was immediate because strychnine death is violent and the bottle was the obvious vehicle; Vaquier was seen handling it afterwards and the bottle, rinsed, still carried traces (the rinsing detail is UNVERIFIED). He had bought the strychnine at a London chemist a month earlier, signing the poison register "J. Wanker" and saying it was for wireless experiments; the chemist recognised him when Vaquier, vain about the attention, posed for newspaper photographers. Police acted on the first report; the poison made that unavoidable. Hanged August 1924. Instructive because it is the pure form of the routine-object attack: the tamperer never has to be present, the victim does the work, and the only thing that needs to be true is that the habit is fixed.

### 3. Herbert Rowse Armstrong, Hay-on-Wye, 1921

A solicitor. His wife Katharine died in February 1921 after a long illness and was buried; the following October his professional rival, the solicitor Oswald Martin, was invited to tea at Armstrong's house and handed a buttered scone with the words "excuse fingers". Martin was violently ill that night and survived. Martin's father-in-law, John Davies, was the local chemist who had sold Armstrong arsenical weedkiller; he and the doctor, Tom Hincks, had already been uneasy about a box of chocolates sent anonymously to the Martins that had made a guest ill. A urine sample went to London and came back showing arsenic. What matters for the story is what happened next: police and the Home Office investigated quietly for two months while Armstrong kept issuing tea invitations, and Martin, knowing what he suspected and knowing how it would sound, had to keep inventing excuses to a colleague he met in the street daily. Arrested 31 December 1921 with a packet of arsenic in his pocket; Katharine exhumed; hanged May 1922. A tea, not a fixed routine of the victim, and poison rather than mechanism; I include it because it is the best documented account of a survivor living alongside the person he believes tried to kill him while nobody official will move. Instructive because it shows the psychological shape of Violet's position exactly, down to the fear of sounding absurd.

### 4. George Joseph Smith, the Brides in the Bath, 1912 to 1914

**This is staging, not a rigged mechanism.** Smith married and drowned three women in lodging-house baths: Bessie Mundy at Herne Bay (July 1912), Alice Burnham at Blackpool (December 1913), Margaret Lofty at Highgate (December 1914). In each case he had taken the woman to a doctor days before complaining of fits or headaches, then "found" her drowned. The physical evidence was nothing: no bruising, no struggle, water on the floor. Doctors and coroners' juries returned misadventure or accidental drowning each time; Smith had bought Mundy's bath on approval and returned it after. Police did not act on any first report because there was nothing to act on. It broke because Alice Burnham's father and the Blackpool landlord each read the News of the World account of Lofty's death and recognised their own story; Detective Inspector Arthur Neil then built the pattern, and Spilsbury's experiments with a woman diver established that lifting the legs sends the head under without a struggle. Hanged 1915. Instructive because it established, for real coroners, that a woman alone in a bath is the most plausible accidental death in a house, which is why the bath is where a fiction should put its mechanism.

### 5. Kenneth Barlow, Bradford, 1957

**Staging with a drug, not a mechanism.** Barlow, a state-enrolled nurse, injected his wife Elizabeth with insulin and put her in the bath, then reported that she had felt ill, gone for a bath and drowned, and that he had tried to lift her out and given artificial respiration. The first officer's scene was a wet woman in a bath and a distressed husband. What did not fit were small domestic things: his pyjamas were dry; there was no water on the bathroom floor; water still lay in the crook of her elbow, meaning she had not been lifted; her pupils were dilated and the bedding was soaked with sweat. The pathologist found two fresh injection sites on each buttock and insulin was recovered from the tissue, the first time that had been done. Convicted, life. Police acted at once, but only because one officer noticed the dry pyjamas. Instructive because it shows what a competent observer actually catches: not the mechanism but the domestic detail the story requires and does not have.

### 6. Reginald Hinks, Bath, 1933

**Staging, not tampering, but it is a gas case.** Hinks married the daughter of James Pullen, an elderly and confused retired builder with money, dismissed the male nurse, and spent months trying to arrange an accident (leaving him on roads, encouraging him to wander). On 1 December 1933 he telephoned the fire brigade to say he had found his father-in-law with his head in the gas oven. Pullen was dead. The evidence was a bruise on the back of the head; Hinks volunteered, before anyone had mentioned it, that if there was a bruise it had happened when he dragged the old man out. He had also called too soon for the gas to have done the job on its own. Hanged 1934. Police acted on the first report because the household history was already known locally. Instructive because it shows two failure modes of the tamperer that a story can use: explaining the evidence before being asked, and mistiming the discovery.

### 7. Sidney Fox, Margate, 1929

**Staging, in a hotel, not a home.** Fox strangled his mother Rosaline in their room at the Hotel Metropole on the night her accident insurance policies were due to expire, then set a fire under her armchair and raised the alarm. The first reading was an old woman overcome by smoke from a fire that had spread from the gas fire. It unravelled on the carpet: there was an unburnt strip between the gas fire and the chair, so the fire could not have travelled; the burning had started under the chair itself (petrol as accelerant is UNVERIFIED). Spilsbury found a bruise on the larynx and no soot in the airways, meaning she was dead before the fire. Hanged 1930. Police did not act until the insurance company queried it. Instructive because the physical tell was not the mechanism but a gap where continuity should have been, which is precisely the kind of "absent that should be present" Violet's eye is built for.

### 8. Katko v. Briney, Iowa, 1967 (decided 1971)

Not intimates, not disguised, and a civil case, included for the reverse lesson. Edward and Bertha Briney rigged a shotgun in a bedroom of an unoccupied farmhouse they owned, wired to fire at the door, to stop break-ins. Marvin Katko broke in to steal old jars and took the charge in the legs. He sued and won (damages in the region of $30,000 including punitive; UNVERIFIED). It worked exactly as built and the evidence was the device. Instructive because a mechanism designed to fire leaves a mechanism, so the only house trap that survives inspection is one that consists of nothing added, only something altered.

### 9. Malcolm Webster, Aberdeenshire 1994 and New Zealand 1999

**Drugging plus staged car fire; not tampering with the house.** Webster sedated his wife Claire Morris with temazepam and drove the car off a road, where it burned; she died; he collected insurance. In 1999 in New Zealand he married Felicity Drumm, insured her, and drugged her repeatedly; a planned crash came to nothing and her family, noticing her unexplained illnesses, forced the issue. The Scottish case was reopened years later and he was convicted in 2011. Nobody suspected the first death: a crash and a fire on a rural road read as what they looked like. Instructive because the second target survived through relatives noticing a pattern in her body, not any evidence in the wreck, and because it shows the tamperer returning to the same method.

That is nine. Cases four to seven and nine are staging, and I have said so in each. The two poisonings, Vaquier and Armstrong, are the permitted domestic-routine ones.

## Part two: applied

One technical note before the candidates, because it affects three of them. "The older kind of gas" in a British-shaped city means iron pipework and painted joints, but unless the story is set before the late 1970s the gas itself is natural gas, which does not poison. It kills by explosion or, through a faulty appliance, by carbon monoxide; the excluded list already rules out the second. Town gas killed in its sleep; natural gas has to be lit. I have written the gas candidate accordingly.

Two candidates assume the bathroom has an electric shower over the bath and two assume she runs a bath from an immersion-heated cylinder; those pairs cannot both be true of one house, but only one candidate will be chosen.

### Candidate A: the shower that makes the taps live

Pattern: Brides in the Bath and Barlow. The bath is where a fit woman's death is written up as a faint and nobody asks a second question.

The event. She comes in off the slip at twenty to six, salt-wet, straight upstairs, steps into the bath in the dark, pulls the shower's isolator cord and reaches for the hot tap. Every metal pipe in the bathroom is at mains voltage and the old cast-iron bath she is standing in is earthed through its lead waste to the iron stack. She is the bridge.

The habit and why only the four know it. The shower straight from the sea is known to everyone who has ever stayed the night or called before seven, which is the four. The second piece of knowledge is what makes it point at them and nobody else: the previous winter the water company relaid Mariner's Row in plastic and her electrician told her the house had lost its earth (this is a real and common fault in old terraces earthed through the water main). She said so at her own kitchen table with all four present; Margo asked what it would cost, Ruby told her to get it done, Liam offered to look, Brad made a joke. She never got it done. The tampering is a single act: inside the shower unit, the incoming earth conductor moved onto the live terminal. In a house with a good earth that would blow the fuse at once. In this house nothing blows, and the metalwork sits live from the moment the isolator is pulled.

How she comes to look. She ran a bead of silicone round the shower unit against the tiles herself, years ago, as straight as a pinstripe because she could not bear the fitter's. Brushing her teeth the night before, she sees the bead is broken along the top and down one side. Not cracked with age, which crazes and lifts; cut, one clean blade line with a square end. She kills the isolator and the fuse, takes the cover off, and sees a green-and-yellow sleeved conductor sitting in the live terminal. She is not an electrician but she knows what green-and-yellow means, everywhere, always.

Why it says nothing to an officer or a tradesman. The electrician who comes says it is lethal and asks who wired it. The unit was in the house when she bought it fifteen years ago. He says: wired wrong from the day it went in, and your lost earth is what finally made it dangerous; you have been lucky. She says she has used it every morning since the street was dug up. He says nobody knows exactly when the earth went, the main was done in sections; perhaps it only failed last month. That cannot be disproved. The officer writes "unsafe installation, no injury" and advises her to have the house rewired. Nobody but Violet knows the silicone was whole.

What she keeps. The cut ribbon of silicone in an envelope, and photographs of the terminal block before she lets anyone touch it.

What would make it fail as story. A reader who knows electrics will ask why the fuse did not blow, so the lost earth has to be planted early, plainly, at that dinner; if the planting is clumsy it reads as set-up rather than life.

### Candidate B: the joint behind the cash tin

Pattern: Backhouse, in that the victim's own routine act fires it, with Vaquier's lesson that the tamperer need never be present.

The event. The old iron gas pipe rises through the kitchen floor inside the base cupboard where the cookie tin lives, and a painted union nut joins it there. Backed off a quarter turn, it weeps gas into the closed cupboard and down through the gap in the floorboards into the void under the kitchen. Two nights later she comes in from the harbour, nose dead from cold water (a swimmer's nose runs and smells nothing for an hour; all four have watched her fail to smell burning toast after a swim, and Ruby has said it in the car), puts the pan on, and strikes a match to the ring for her egg. The kitchen and the void go up together.

The habit and why only the four know it. The egg at the counter every morning and the older cooker with no ignition, lit with a match from the box on the sill. The tin cupboard, which every one of the four has seen opened: Margo does her books from it, Brad has been paid out of it, Liam has borrowed from it, Ruby has watched her count it. And the deaf nose after the sea. Nobody else in the city has been in that kitchen at a quarter to six.

How she comes to look. Saturday is tin day. She opens the cupboard for the money and her eye goes, as it always does, to the pipe she painted cream ten years ago, because the skin of paint that ran unbroken across the union has cracked in a ring, and two flats of the nut show bright metal where a spanner has bitten through the paint. Only then does she smell it, faintly, evening nose working. She shuts the gas off at the meter and opens the windows. Paint does not crack in a ring by itself, and a spanner does not visit a joint nobody has touched in a decade.

Why it says nothing to an officer or a tradesman. The emergency gas engineer finds a weeping joint on old iron pipe, which he sees weekly, remakes it, and files an uncontrolled escape. He notes someone has had a spanner on it and asks if she has had work done. She has not. Then it was the last man, he says, and the paint cracked with the seasons. She has lived there fifteen years; he shrugs. A police officer, if she rang one, would hear "gas leak, fixed" and there is no offence in a leak.

What she keeps. Photographs of the cracked paint ring and the bright flats before the engineer's spanner adds its own marks, and the flake of paint skin that came away.

What would make it fail as story. Every reader knows gas smells. The numb nose and the sub-floor accumulation have to carry it, and if the reader does not believe she would walk into that kitchen and strike a match, the whole thing is a smell she should have noticed.

### Candidate C: the brass switch in the passage

Pattern: Vaquier. The object she reaches for first every morning, altered once.

The event. She comes through the front door dripping at twenty to six, kicks her wet sandals off on the mat, and walks barefoot over the quarry tiles of the passage to the old brass dolly switch. Inside it, the rubber insulation on the live has been cut back and the bare copper folded to bear against the back of the brass plate. Wet salt hand, wet feet on damp tile, current across the chest.

The habit and why only the four know it. That she comes in barefoot and wet in the dark, and that the passage light is the first thing she touches, is known only to people who have seen her arrive, and only the four have. Ruby has followed her in on a Tuesday. Liam has let himself in behind her. The switch is on an unearthed lighting circuit with the original rubber wiring, which anyone who has changed a bulb in that house knows.

How she comes to look. She comes home that evening, dry, in shoes, and stops in the passage because the switch plate is a degree off vertical and she cannot leave a crooked line alone. When she goes to straighten it she sees that the hairline of paint she cut in around its edge years ago is broken all the way round, cleanly, and one screw slot carries a fresh bright scratch. She pulls the lighting fuse and takes the plate off. Perished rubber crumbles and leaves ragged powdery edges; this rubber is cut square with a blade and the copper is bright.

Why it says nothing to an officer or a tradesman. An electrician says old rubber wiring, it perishes, you need a rewire, and if he notices the clean end he says someone stripped it back too far once and it has crept. He has no way to date a cut. An officer sees a fifty-year-old switch. There is no injury and nothing missing.

What she keeps. A photograph of the cut end against the crumbled rubber further along the same wire, and the paint chip.

What would make it fail as story. It is a smaller mechanism than A and a reader may not feel the certainty of death without the wet feet and salt spelled out; and it shares a family with A, so the story cannot have both a wired shower and a wired switch without the second looking like a repeat.

### Candidate D: the red wheels

Pattern: Katko. The house's own fixture turned into the weapon, with nothing added.

The event. The kitchen has a solid-fuel stove with a back boiler feeding the hot cylinder upstairs, an old gravity system a long-dead plumber bodged with a gate valve on each pipe in the airing cupboard. She comes in from the harbour and opens the stove right up to bring the banked fire on fast while she fries the egg two feet from it. With both wheels shut the water in the boiler cannot move; within the hour it boils, the old boiler splits, and steam and boiling water blow through the firebox into the kitchen where she is standing. This is a real class of accident, the disused or isolated back boiler; fire services warn about it because it kills.

The habit and why only the four know it. The stove opened up hard first thing after a swim in the cold months, and the standing warning. The plumber who serviced it told her never to touch the red wheels, and she repeated it to each of the four in the way she repeats things, so that "don't touch the red wheels" is a phrase between them. Nobody else has heard it. The tampering is one visit and one intention, two wheels turned by one hand; I am arguing this clears rule eight, which is aimed at two coordinated mechanisms, not two turns of a wrist.

How she comes to look. She gets her towel from the airing cupboard every morning after the swim and before she touches the stove. She painted the wheels cream with the wall years ago because they were ugly, and one wheel has a chipped spoke that has sat at twelve o'clock for as long as she has looked at it. It is at four. The paint skin across the spindle has cracked in a ring. She does not know what shutting them does, but she knows she was told never to, and that they were not shut yesterday. She does not open the stove up. She rings the plumber.

Why it says nothing to an officer or a tradesman. The plumber says someone has shut them or they have worked shut; if he is good he goes quiet for a moment and then says you were lucky, and removes the valves altogether, which destroys the evidence. An officer hears "a couple of valves in an airing cupboard" and cannot be made to understand why that is an attempt on a life without a paragraph he will not sit through.

What she keeps. A photograph of the chipped spoke at four o'clock with the paint ring, taken before the plumber's spanner.

What would make it fail as story. The death needs one line of physics the reader has to accept on trust, and it sits close to the excluded stove exclusions; it is not carbon monoxide and not a damper, but a reader who has just been told "no stove" may feel cheated, and the added habit of opening the stove up hard in the morning is mine.

### Candidate E: the thermostat and the faint

Pattern: Smith's own cover story and Barlow's. "She fainted in the bath" is the account coroners accepted in every one of those cases.

The event. She runs a hot bath straight from the sea. The immersion heater's thermostat has been turned from its long-standing setting to its stop, and the water is far hotter than her numbed hands read it. Getting into very hot water within minutes of cold immersion drops the blood pressure hard (Ruby has told her this in the car more than once, calling it rewarming collapse); she goes under in her own bath and does not come up.

The habit and why only the four know it. The bath at full heat immediately after cold water, against nursing advice, is a running argument between her and Ruby that the others have laughed at. The immersion heater cap is in the airing cupboard that all four have been sent to for towels.

How she comes to look. The evening before, the kitchen hot tap steams as she washes up, which it has never done, and she notices because she notices everything the house does that it did not do yesterday. In the airing cupboard she finds the thermostat cap's screw slot no longer vertical (she sets every screw slot in the house upright; I am adding that habit, and it is a signwriter's habit). She had dabbed a paint tick on the dial when the electrician set it. The tick is at the stop. Thermostats drift; they do not drift to the stop overnight and they do not turn their own screws.

Why it says nothing to an officer or a tradesman. The electrician says someone has turned it up, perhaps you did and forgot, and turns it down. An officer hears "hot water".

What she keeps. A photograph of the dial with the paint tick at the stop, and the cap.

What would make it fail as story. It is a bet, not a certainty: a fit forty-four-year-old more likely yelps and runs the cold, and the reader knows it, so it fails rule seven unless Ruby's warning is planted heavily enough to make the reader believe the faint.

### Candidate F: the wrong bulb

Pattern: Fox, a fire that reads as an accident, and Webster, sleep as the enabler. I say plainly that this is a cousin of the excluded clothes peg by the heater; it differs in that nothing combustible is placed, her own shade is bent onto her own bulb, and the thing she would notice is light, which she cannot help noticing.

The event. She reads the last page first in bed and falls asleep with the lamp on, which she always does after cold water and which Liam has told her off for. Her 40-watt pearl bulb has been replaced by a 150-watt and the wire ring of the parchment shade bent inward so the shade rests against the glass. The shade catches an hour after she is asleep; she sleeps hard; the bedroom is at the top of an old terrace.

The habit and why only the four know it. Falling asleep reading with the light on is known to people who have stayed the night or been let in the next morning to find it still burning: the four.

How she comes to look. She switches the lamp on and the room is wrong. The line where light meets shadow on the ceiling has moved, the colour is whiter, the shade is scorching already. She looks at the shade ring. Wire rings do not bend inward on their own, and a bulb does not change itself.

Why it says nothing to an officer or a tradesman. You have put the wrong bulb in, love. There is no tradesman to call.

What she keeps. The bulb and the bent ring.

What would make it fail as story. It is probabilistic and small; a reader may feel she would have smelt scorching and woken, and rule seven wants certainty.

### Candidate G: the lamp on the kitchen table

Pattern: Vaquier again, the object at the centre of a fixed routine, handled for hours.

The event. Her old brass work lamp, two-core flex, no earth. At the bulb holder the live has been pulled from its terminal and trapped under the holder's brass shell, so the whole arm is live when the lamp is on. She adjusts that arm every few minutes to kill the glare on a layout. On a night when she stands up to rinse a brush with one hand still on the arm, current runs hand to hand through the chest via the sink and the earthed cold pipe.

The habit and why only the four know it. The late nights at the kitchen table, the constant fidgeting with the lamp, the brushes rinsed at the sink mid-job: all four have sat opposite her during it, and Brad learned to work that way from her.

How she comes to look. The lamp's flex cable clamp screw, which she set vertical, is off vertical, and the brass shell of the holder sits a millimetre proud where it did not. She unplugs it and opens it. The live's end is not frayed but cut square and deliberately folded to the shell.

Why it says nothing to an officer or a tradesman. Old lamp, old flex, a wire has come adrift and shorted to the case; happens all the time; throw it away.

What she keeps. The lamp.

What would make it fail as story. A shock from a lamp is a shock, not a death, unless her other hand is on the sink, and the story has to arrange that; readers feel arrangement.

### Candidate H: the cap that was not there

Pattern: Katko, a trap in a room entered on a routine, and Fox's "absent that should be present".

The event. The immersion heater's terminal cap has been removed and the live tail dressed outward so that it stands a hand's breadth from the top shelf of the airing cupboard where the towels are. She reaches into that dark cupboard every morning with a wet salt hand and bare wet feet on the landing lino.

The habit and why only the four know it. The towel from the top shelf in the dark, first thing, before any light is on, because the landing bulb dazzles her after the sea. All four have been sent to that shelf.

How she comes to look. Putting laundry away on Sunday she finds the cap sitting on her towel stack with its retaining screw placed inside it. A cap that has fallen is on the floor with the screw lost; a cap with its screw tidily inside it was put there by a hand.

Why it says nothing to an officer or a tradesman. The plumber says the last man left it off and you never noticed in the dark; he screws it back on.

What she keeps. A photograph of the cap on the towels with the screw inside it.

What would make it fail as story. It bets on a fumble; her hand has to find a terminal in the dark, and a reader will not believe she dies until she does.

## Forced ranking

1. **A, the shower.** It beats everything below because it is the only one where death is certain the moment she pulls the cord, the bath supplies the coroner's verdict for free, and the lost earth makes the four the only people on earth who knew the house would not protect her.
2. **B, the joint behind the tin.** Beats C because the death is unarguable to any reader and the tell, a cracked skin of her own paint, is the most Violet detail in the set; it loses to A only on the smell.
3. **C, the brass switch.** Beats D because it needs no physics lesson at all, only wet feet and a wet hand, and the crooked plate is the purest expression of who she is; it loses to B because it is the smaller image.
4. **D, the red wheels.** Beats E because it kills for certain if she opens the stove up, and the shared warning phrase binds the four tightly; it loses to C because the reader has to take the boiler on trust and it brushes the stove exclusions.
5. **E, the thermostat.** Beats F because at least the mechanism is aimed squarely at the one thing about her the four know best, the hot bath after cold water; it loses to D because the faint is a bet.
6. **F, the wrong bulb.** Beats G because a sleeping woman in a burning bedroom is a death no reader questions; it loses to E because it is a peg cousin and the ignition is probabilistic.
7. **G, the work lamp.** Beats H because the object is genuinely hers and handled for hours; it loses to F because the death needs her other hand placed by the author.
8. **H, the missing cap.** Last because the evidence detail is lovely and the death is a lottery.
