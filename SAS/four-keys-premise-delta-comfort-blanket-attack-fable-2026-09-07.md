# ADVERSARIAL REVIEW: PREMISE DELTA v0.2, "THE COMFORT BLANKET"

## Front 1. Violet's engine

**Strongest objection.** The delta says Violet never accuses anyone, and then her true letter accuses the four as a class. "If I'm dead and it wasn't my stairs or my heart, then I wasn't imagining it, and someone with my spare key has been in my house; you four know where the spare lives, which is why it's you reading this." That is a pool accusation with a posthumous trigger. A woman whose first reading is "it has started" does not write a conditional on her own death. Dementia at forty-four does not kill you in weeks; violence does. The padlock, the lodged letters and "open this if I am dead" are all driven by the second reading, the one the delta says she does not reach for. So the engine has not changed. v8.1's fear of the four is still the thing that makes her lodge letters with a solicitor; it has been moved one paragraph down and stripped of the "I think" hedge that made it cohere.

**The shame.** Shame about her mind explains not telling the four she is frightened. It does not explain giving a locker key to each of the four people who know where the spare lives, one of whom she has just posited as an intruder. A frightened woman writing "someone with my spare key has been in my house" and then posting a key to everyone who has the spare is not ashamed, she is careless, and the text has told us she is the opposite.

**"She never saw a doctor."** Weaker than the next point but real: her livelihood is her hand, her mother went at fifty, and she is now asking a café whether a panel is hers. The professional whose whole standing is judgement of a hand would want the one thing shame does not stop her getting, which is a private answer. But the deeper break is the panel itself. Gilding a panel in her alphabet "better than she has ever done it" is days of work and consumes stock: leaf, size, board. Dementia does not make a master better at her craft, and a fugue does not buy gold leaf. She is the best judge of a hand on the coast. She would check her stock, check her order book and know inside a minute that she did not make it. "I don't know whether I made it" is credible from a woman about a loose tin lid. It is not credible from this woman about that object.

**"She asked the café but not Brad."** She trained Brad for seven years. He is the only other hand on the coast that can do her alphabet. Asking two other signwriters "whose hand is this" and not asking the one she taught is not shame, because the question to strangers is the same question and reveals nothing about her mind. It is plot convenience wearing shame's coat. Worse: the two other signwriters, professionals shown a panel in Violet Moore's alphabet by Violet Moore, would answer "your boy, surely." That is the natural answer of anyone in the trade, and it ends the story on Day minus 40 (see Front 6).

**Severity: fatal as written.** The delta's justification is that the accusation is removed from Violet. It is not; only the hedge is.

**What would have to be true.** Her locker letter would have to end at "I don't know whether I did that" with no death conditional and no pool, which removes her reason for a padlock and a posthumous trigger; and the panel would have to be an object she could plausibly doubt authoring, which a gilded piece in her alphabet cannot be.

## Front 2. The kept letter

**Strongest objection.** Brad's character, as ruled, is a documentary criminal: a PO box, a forged hand, a dead deckhand's name, an exit built on paper that never points back. The genuine letter is the one page on earth that proves the locker was swapped, and only the man with workshop access and a dawn key could have swapped it. Keeping the panel is at least a maker's vanity. Keeping her letter is a second trophy with no value to him and total value to a prosecutor. "As he keeps the panel" is not a reason, it is an analogy, and the delta gives nothing else. A man who forged her hand for a day at her own bench and then walked into the sea on paper does not carry her confession to his lodgings and keep it nine months.

**Is it coherent with documents never carrying his face?** Partly. A random landlady finding a letter in a dead stranger's hand connects it to nothing; that is fair to the delta. But that concession cuts the other way: the letter is inert until his identity is known, and evidence that is inert until the case is solved is epilogue, not plot. Its only function is to let Ally read it in the close.

**The day before the arrest, and by whom.** Two candidates. First, Ally herself, or a listener, on the day the deckhand's address is known and before police act. A podcaster who has spent ten weeks on this and has an address does not wait a day. The script would have to physically prevent the protagonist from doing the obvious thing. Second, and this is the one that breaks the close: once he is arrested under the deckhand's name, the room is searched by police and the letter is seized as evidence in a murder. It is not shown to a podcaster before trial, in any jurisdiction the setting resembles. So "found in his room the day he is arrested" and "Ally reads it in the close" cannot both be true through any lawful route. Either she reads it before the arrest, which means she was in a murder suspect's room, or she does not read it.

**Severity: serious for Brad, fatal for the close as sketched.**

**What would have to be true.** Brad would need a reason to keep it stronger than his reason to burn it, established and paid earlier (leverage, or an inability to destroy her hand that the story has shown us), and the letter's route to Ally would have to run through someone entitled to hold it before police do, which the delta's own timing forbids.

## Front 3. The room on the Monday night

**Strongest objection.** The bare note makes the forgery less believable, not more, and it does so through a question Brad cannot answer because he did not control it. The four keys were posted before he could forge. The key note says "these four keys are the only ones" and "it's probably nothing." The locker letter says "I know now" and "one of you." The question one of them asks, and the delta has not answered: "If she knew which of us, why did she post the killer a key?" In v8.1 "I don't know which" answered that. Here the forgery's certainty and the key distribution contradict each other on night one, in front of an accountant and a nurse, with no counterweight.

**The second unanswered question, and it is worse.** The object. The board was in her hall; she died on the stairs beside it. Brad could not have put it in a padlocked locker "the night he killed her" because he had no key until the Monday. So it went in with the forgery. That means the board left her hall at some point in the two weeks between death and keys, when the house was entered by police, by the coroner's people, by whoever tidied. Ruby or Liam will know whether it was still in the hall after she died. If it was, then one of four keyholders moved it, and moved it before tonight. "These four keys are the only ones" turns the object from a relic into a timestamp pointing at the room.

**The third, and it belongs to Brad.** Under v8.1 all four already held "one of you is planning to kill me" before he opened the locker; the murder story existed and he steered it. Under v0.2 the true letter exonerates everyone: a woman doubting her mind. His safest move is to leave it, or substitute nothing. Instead he manufactures a murder accusation inside his own circle, containing a money claim that one person in the room knows to be false, and thereby turns a closed accident into the story that eventually brings Ally. The forgery is now against his interest. The delta has not asked why he writes it.

**Severity: fatal.** The room is the whole middle, and its founding night has three open questions the ruled version did not have.

**What would have to be true.** The forgery's register would have to be consistent with a woman who posted all four keys; the board's whereabouts between death and keys would have to be accounted for against every person who entered the house; and Brad would need a reason to convert "poor Violet" into "murder" that outweighs leaving it alone.

## Front 4. The first minute

**Strongest objection.** "The note didn't say why" is an announced withholding, and an announced withholding is a delay. The listener is walked through post, key, note, names, locker, together, night, door before the sting. That is logistics in audio, and it lands the accusation later than the ruled benchmark (keys about 0:50, accusation on p01_04), which was already a constraint fought for. The sting itself ("this one said she knew") is good and the object button ("neither one will say what that object was") is good; the problem is the corridor to them.

**A voice problem inside it.** "It said one of the four of them had killed her." A letter written while alive cannot say that. Ally's voice spec is precision, and a careful listener catches the tense in the first minute. "She knew which" is then a promise the forgery does not keep, since it names no one; either the open says so, or the listener feels cheated by chapter 2.

**Severity: serious, and testable rather than arguable.**

**What would have to be true.** A read-aloud against the ruled first-tap and keys timings would have to pass, and the sting would have to be phrased as what the letter actually says.

## Front 5. Chapter 7

**Strongest objection.** It is a list. v8.1's turn was one line that re-hears every prior scene: dead women don't get surer. The five items here are each an inference, none flips the frame on its own, and several are compromised. The kind line confirms a suspicion the listener must already hold; it does not create one. "Nobody alive ever saw Brad write" is unsupportable: his hand is on shopfronts across the city, and he sat at Ruby's table for weeks working a letter with pen in hand. "She bought a padlock" is a deflation, not a reversal, and it points away from the four entirely, not toward Brad. Margo's money is unchanged and was never the turn. That leaves the panel against the board, which is a genuine physical contradiction and the only candidate for a turn, and it is available to anyone who asks the café on Day 1 (Front 6).

**An irony the delta missed.** It has kept a certainty gap without noticing: "it's probably nothing" in the key note against "I know now" in the locker. But it is a weaker gap, because "probably nothing" reads as reassurance to loved ones and dissolves under the first sympathetic reading.

**Severity: serious.** The chapter that was the spine's strongest beat becomes its weakest.

**What would have to be true.** One item would have to be elevated to a reversal that reframes the previous six chapters, and its lateness would have to be earned against the fact that its witnesses were available from the first week.

## Front 6. The five new facts, earliest competent action

**The kept letter.** Earliest competent action is Brad's own, on the Monday: burn it. In-story, the earliest ender is not the letter but what sits beside it. Ally must describe the panel publicly by the time of the panel-against-board beat. From that episode on, any person who has been in the lodger's room (landlady for the annual gas check, a tradesman, a visitor) and has heard a nationally distributed podcast describe a gilded panel in a dead woman's alphabet, ends the story that week. Serious.

**The mother's early dementia.** Ruby. Ruby is a nurse; she knows the mother went at fifty; Violet asked her in the car what early dementia looked like; the coroner recorded a fall on the stairs. A nurse in a kitchen holding "I know now" from that woman says, on night one or night two, "her mother went early, she asked me about it, she fell down her own stairs, this is a frightened woman's letter, not a witness's." That reading discounts the forgery's authority in the first week and dissolves the six weeks at the table. The texture the delta added to give Violet an interior hands Ruby the means to end the room. Fatal to the middle unless Ruby cannot make the connection, which for a nurse means the car question is cut.

**Nothing on her medical record.** A null cannot be acted on, so no early ender. But note the delta assumes Ruby reads a dead friend's record on Day 2; that is an audited breach that puts Ruby in front of information governance and then the police within a week of the funeral. Cosmetic to the plot, not to Ruby.

**The packet of keys.** Liam, Monday night. He works in a boatyard; he handles padlocks daily; he looks at four identical factory keys and a boxed padlock and says "she didn't choose four of us, this is a four-key pack from the chandlery." The chapter 7 beat lands on Day 1 in the mouth of the man in the room best placed to say it, or Liam is written not to notice his own trade. Serious. A second edge: having posted all four keys via the solicitor, Violet could not reopen her own locker from the day she lodged them, which no one writes a "comfort blanket" to do.

**The way she asked round town.** The two other signwriters, Day minus 40. Violet Moore shows a professional a panel in Violet Moore's alphabet and asks whose hand it is. The competent answer is "your apprentice." She then knows, six weeks before she dies, that Brad has been in her roof, and the whole story changes. Failing that, the café and cinema on Day 1 of the podcast: Ally asks what Violet was doing in her last weeks and hears "carrying a gold panel round asking whose it was." The locker held a boat board. The spine's X surfaces in chapter 1 or 2, and chapters 3 to 6 are played by people who already hold the contradiction. Fatal unless the signwriters are removed or written as ignorant of her only pupil, and the café witness is somehow unreachable for six chapters.

## Verdict

**REJECT, high confidence.** The finding that drives it: the delta does not do what it claims. Violet's true letter still accuses the four as a class ("someone with my spare key... you four know where the spare lives, which is why it's you reading this"), and it needs a posthumous trigger that only fear of violence supplies, so the engine is v8.1's fear of the four with the "I think" hedge removed. That hedge was load-bearing three times over: it explained posting a key to the killer, it motivated Brad to forge at all rather than leave an exonerating letter alone, and it was the chapter 7 turn. v0.2 spends all three to buy a comfort-blanket framing the text itself does not sustain, and the facts it admits to pay for the framing (a nurse who was asked about early dementia, two signwriters shown the panel, a boatyard man handed a four-key pack) each arm a competent character to end the episode in its first week. v8.1 stands; the specific costs it was trying to remove have not been shown here to be greater than what this delta breaks.
