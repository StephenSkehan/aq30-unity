  1. Violet’s engine

Strongest objection: family history explains why Violet fears dementia. It does not explain this machinery. She mistrusts her own memory enough to contemplate cognitive decline, yet trusts that same memory enough to document two incidents, infer their significance after her death and direct four people into an investigation. Her uncertainty is invoked whenever action would expose the plot and discarded whenever action must preserve it.

The shame does not scale. She is supposedly too ashamed to speak privately to four people who love her, including a nurse, but she carries the panel around town and asks a café, a cinema and two signwriters whether the work might be hers. In a small city, that is not secrecy. It is disclosure to the people most likely to gossip.

“She never saw a doctor” is also unsupported convenience. Seeing a doctor would not make a diagnosis public, and fitness would not prevent a confidential assessment. More damagingly, Violet already asks Ruby what early dementia looks like. Total silence is therefore false. A competent nurse and close friend would not ignore that question merely because Violet changes the subject.

Excluding Brad is naked plot preservation. He is the other signwriter, her pupil of seven years and the most qualified person she knows to judge the alphabet. Asking two other signwriters while deliberately avoiding him requires Violet to suspect him, fear his reaction or distrust his judgement. The delta permits none of those explanations.

Fatal. Violet’s silence, Brad’s delayed discovery and the entire posthumous device depend on behaviour the stated shame does not support.

It would be answered only if Violet had an established pattern of refusing medical help, using sealed posthumous arrangements to manage uncertainty, preventing Ruby from recognising the dementia question as personal, and seeking outside opinions without revealing either the panel’s origin or her fear. The delta establishes none of this.

  1. The kept letter

Strongest objection: Brad preserves the most incriminating object in the story for no stated reason. The genuine letter proves that Violet did not write the accusation, records facts he removed, points to unauthorized access and can be compared directly with his forgery. Destroying it costs him nothing. Keeping it gives him nothing.

This contradicts the antagonist already built around documentary absence. He hides ownership, moves money through concealment, constructs allegations without naming himself, disappears under another man’s identity and relies on records never carrying his face. He then transports the document capable of dismantling all of that four hundred miles and stores it in someone else’s house.

On Day 332, the landlady could enter the room for an ordinary inspection, maintenance issue or concern about her lodger and find it. “Where the landlady does not dust” is not security. It confirms that she enters the room. If she recognises Violet’s name from coverage, she can contact police before the planned arrest. If she does not, the letter remains exposed to discovery through rent trouble, illness, fire inspection, cleaning, theft or Brad’s sudden disappearance.

Fatal. The close now depends on Brad behaving against his defining survival logic every day for nine months.

It would be answered only if the letter served an established psychological or practical need powerful enough to outweigh its evidential danger, and if its hiding place were genuinely inaccessible to the landlady and every other foreseeable entrant. Neither condition exists.

  1. The room on Monday night

The bare note makes the forged letter less believable. It says all four must attend together. The forged letter then says Violet knew which one of those four would kill her. The immediate question is:

If she knew which of us would kill her, why did she give that person a key and order the other three to meet them at an isolated locker?

The delta does not answer that. It also does not answer why Violet refuses to name the killer she claims to know. Instead, she supplies indirect financial clues, invites the supposed killer to inspect them and prohibits the innocent three from seeking police protection. That is not cautious ambiguity. It is suicidal nonsense wearing Violet’s handwriting.

The first delta failed because the genuine letter contradicted the forgery chronologically. This version removes the chronology but creates a contradiction of purpose. The two documents still cannot plausibly come from one rational author.

Fatal. The other three must accept the forged letter for the episode to continue, but its instructions disprove its claim.

It would be answered only if the room could explain why Violet knowingly assembled three innocent people with her identified killer, withheld the killer’s name and denied them police protection. No such explanation is supplied.

  1. The first minute

“The note didn’t say why” is a delay, not a hook. It withholds the premise while the listener receives postal logistics, four keys, a locker instruction and four names. The listener is asked to retain the suspect list before being given a reason to care who any of them are.

The locked door creates mild curiosity. The accusation creates the true-crime engine. Moving that engine behind the door means the opening spends attention merely reaching the point the existing version already reached. The later line is also weakened by its own absurdity: Violet supposedly knew which friend killed her but did not name that person.

“Only two are still alive” and the concealed object provide jeopardy once reached, but they do not prove the preceding delay earns its time. The currently ruled opening has positive reader evidence. This recut has an author’s expectation that readers will wait.

Serious. The accusation may still arrive within the minute, but the approach to it is weaker, more administrative and burdened with names before stakes.

It would be answered only if blind listeners hearing the exact recut identify the unexplained note and locker as sufficient reasons to continue, understand the four names and do not experience the delayed accusation as withheld information.

  1. Chapter 7

This is a list, not a turn.

The kind line is subjective author leakage. The wrong object in the locker shows that something changed, but not who changed it or whether the letter changed with it. Nobody having seen Brad write proves nothing. Margo’s money may expose an error, a lie or Violet’s misunderstanding. The four-key retail packet removes symbolism from the keys but says nothing about authorship.

These facts attack different propositions. They do not converge on one necessary conclusion:

  • The kind line concerns tone.
  • The panel concerns the locker’s contents.
  • Brad’s hidden skill concerns opportunity.
  • Margo’s money concerns factual accuracy.
  • The key packet concerns Violet’s reason for having four keys.

Ally can announce that the letter is “probably not hers,” but the evidence does not force that result. The scene therefore risks becoming a detective speech that converts accumulated oddities into certainty by authority. Worse, several of the oddities are available on Monday night. Delaying their interpretation makes Ally look perceptive only by making everyone else incurious.

Fatal. Chapter 7 no longer contains a clean contradiction capable of turning the case. It contains five debatable observations arranged to resemble one.

It would be answered only if the combined evidence compelled a single inference unavailable earlier, and that inference materially changed what Ally and the player could do. The current list does neither.

  1. The five new facts

The kept letter: The earliest competent action occurs on Monday morning. Brad destroys it immediately after extracting what he needs. That eliminates the intended close. If he instead keeps it, discovery can occur on any day after he enters the lodging, including Day 332 when the landlady accesses the room. Either competent action breaks the planned timing.

The mother: The earliest competent action occurs when Violet asks Ruby what early dementia looks like. Ruby knows Violet’s family history and does not accept a subject change from a frightened close friend. She asks what Violet has noticed, recommends assessment or follows up later. Any genuine follow-up threatens to expose the panel, the tin and Violet’s reason for secrecy before her death.

Nothing on her record: The earliest competent action occurs on Day 2. Ruby either lacks authority to inspect a dead friend’s medical record, making the discovery improper, or she has a legitimate route and knows that one empty record does not establish that Violet never sought help elsewhere. She contacts the relevant doctor, family or coroner. Treating absence as conclusive is not competent action.

The packet of keys: The earliest competent action occurs as soon as the notes arrive. Every recipient has independent access to unexplained material left by a dead friend. Any one of Liam, Ruby or Margo can visit first, secure the locker, document the lock or contact the solicitor or police before the joint meeting. The genuine letter is then recovered before Brad replaces it. The packet does not merely explain four keys. It gives four people the same opportunity the plot exploits only for Brad.

The way Violet asked around town: The earliest competent action occurs during her first inquiry. A café owner, cinema worker or signwriter asks why Violet has not shown the panel to Brad, the pupil who knows her alphabet best, or tells one of her close friends that Violet is questioning whether she made something she cannot remember. The delta already depends on town gossip reaching Brad. It cannot decree that the same gossip never reaches Ruby, Liam or Margo.

Fatal. The five facts do not merely add texture. Each opens an earlier action that either exposes Violet’s fear, preserves the genuine evidence, invalidates a supporting inference or removes the final reveal.

REJECT. High confidence. The decisive finding is the Monday-night contradiction: Violet allegedly knows which friend will kill her, yet gives that person access and orders three innocent friends to meet the known killer at the evidence locker without police. The forged letter defeats itself at the moment it is read, so the room cannot credibly believe it and the episode cannot reach its intended middle.
