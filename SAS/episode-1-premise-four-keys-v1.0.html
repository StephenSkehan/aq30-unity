1. THE PREMISE, AS RULED

A woman is murdered. Her death is recorded as an accident, so there is no investigation.

Four of her friends receive keys after she dies, under an arrangement she made while she was frightened. The keys open one cupboard, where she hid proof that one of the four was planning to kill her.

★ By the time they get there the cupboard is empty. One of them arrived first. They cannot prove anything and they cannot go to the police with an empty cupboard, and each of them now knows that one of the other three took it.

They start meeting. They start working out which.

★★ Then one of the four disappears. A car somewhere coastal, a phone left behind, no remains. Presumed dead.

★★★ And then, after he is already gone, a second one is murdered.

The public account is that an unknown party took one friend and later killed another. The two who are left know something the police do not: it was one of the four. And the fourth appears to have been taken before the last murder, which seems to clear him.

So there are two of them left, and each believes the other did it.

★ He is alive. Vanishing first was not an escape. It was an alibi for a murder he had not committed yet.

Ally comes in after all of it. She does not prevent anything. What her podcast establishes is that the man everybody has been mourning was never harmed, and that he relocated.

2. THE SEQUENCE

# What happens Who knows
1 She is killed. Recorded as an accident Him
2 The keys go out under her own arrangement The four
3 The cupboard is empty. One of them got there first The four
4 They meet. They start working it out The four
5 ★ One of them comes very close to the truth ★ Him, and this is the trigger
6 He disappears. Car, phone, no body. Presumed dead Nobody
7 ★ He kills her. She was chosen because of step 5 Nobody
8 Public account: a stranger took one and killed another Everybody
9 Two left, each certain it is the other The two
10 Ally arrives —

★ Steps 5 to 7 are the ruling that defines him. He needed a keyholder to die to complete the alibi. He chose that keyholder because she had come very, very close.

What that hands the episode for free:

  • The woman who died was the best of the four at seeing it.
  • ★ The two survivors are the two who were furthest from the truth.
  • They were spared because they were not good enough, and she died because she was. Neither will ever say that to the other, and it sits underneath every conversation they have.

3. THE THREE CONDITIONS, ALL CLOSED

A. Her death was not treated as a murder at the time. Otherwise the arrangement goes straight to police and they open the cupboard first. A solicitor holding a murder victim's instruction hands it over, and any version where they do not is "unbelievable incompetence". Recorded as an accident. No investigation, so nobody for the keys to go to.

B. The cupboard is already empty. An innocent person who opens it takes the proof to the police within the hour and the episode ends at lead one. What survives is not evidence, it is the certainty that one of the people in the room took it. ⚠ Reject any version where a lock needs all four keys. That is a puzzle-box device and puzzle-box thinking dropped the stinger ceiling from 8.5 to 7.5.

C. Somebody alive is at risk. ★ His safety rests entirely on being dead. The podcast is the only thing that can un-kill him. The proof that he is alive is a person, not a record: somebody in his new life knows him under another name and has no idea who he used to be. The moment the podcast describes him, that person becomes the one in danger — and Ally cannot stop without abandoning the case.

★ Police cannot protect her, and it is not a contrivance: nobody knows who she is.

4. WHY FOUR AND NOT FIVE

Stephen's ruling, and it improved the structure rather than just trimming it.

  • Three survivors is a committee. Two is a relationship, and it is inescapable — every conversation in the episode is between two people who each believe the other murdered their friend.
  • A reader can hold two suspects. Five names is a list; two is a question.
  • ★★ It hides the real answer behind a live one. With five and a missing man, an audience guesses the vanished one did it in ten seconds. With two people actively accusing each other, there is a gripping surface question sitting on top of the true one. The survivors' mutual suspicion is the misdirection.
  • Ally becomes the only neutral person either of them can talk to. Two accounts to weigh, and what she does with what each tells her in private is a decision engine on its own.

6. LENGTH AND SESSION STRUCTURE, Stephen-ruled 2026-08-28

The episode is 4 to 5 hours of play, in roughly 30-minute sessions, across a week or more. Eight to ten sessions. Not a single-session story.

Why, and it is not about content volume: a story that finishes in one sitting makes the merge board a toll booth. The alignment audit's rank-one open finding is that nothing demonstrates merging changes what a player believes, and a single-session story guarantees it never will. Spread across a week, each session buys a real turn and the player's investment compounds.

★★ And this premise gets something no other candidate would have: the session boundary and the podcast episode boundary can be the same object.

Ally publishes at the end of a session. The player closes the app. When they return they see what that publication did, because in this story every episode narrows his search for the woman Ally is trying to protect. The gap between sessions stops being dead air and becomes the thing happening to her. It explains the interruption diegetically and makes the danger accumulate at exactly the rate the player plays.

Sixteen leads over eight to ten sessions is about two leads a session.

⚠ Costs to accept now: 16 leads at 4 to 5 hours, times four episodes, is 16 to 20 hours of content with VO and art behind it. That is a game, not a slice. And session one plays by different rules — a fifth of installs die inside two minutes — so the first thirty minutes get designed against a separate brief.
