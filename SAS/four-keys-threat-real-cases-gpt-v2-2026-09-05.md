## Part one: the real world

1. In Cardiff, Wales, in 1993, property developer Peter Ellis was convicted of attempting to murder his wife, Lisa Ellis. He connected part of the bath assembly to the household electricity supply so that the bathwater and metal fittings could carry current. Lisa was reading a magazine in the bath when she saw a blue flash and felt her leg go numb; she escaped because she did not touch the tap, radiator or towel rail. The attack relied on knowing that she took baths and remained in them reading. The melted metal plug chain was the immediate physical evidence, while Ellis tried to explain the incident as static electricity. Lisa called police, but the available reports do not say how the first officer assessed the chain or what was found behind the bath, so those details are UNVERIFIED. Ellis was convicted and imprisoned; the prosecution argued that a death could have appeared to be a heart attack. For fiction, the case shows how a tiny damaged fitting can be more alarming to the intended victim than the invisible apparatus behind it. Peter and Lisa Ellis, The Independent

2. In Fortaleza, Brazil, in 1983, Marco Antônio Heredia Viveiros allegedly tried to electrocute his wife, Maria da Penha Maia Fernandes, while she was bathing and recovering from an earlier attempt on her life. The Inter-American Commission records the bath electrocution attempt but does not describe the wiring, switch, conductor or physical trace, so all such mechanical details are UNVERIFIED. It depended on her need for help and her predictable bathing routine during convalescence, which her husband controlled closely. The official account says the judicial investigation began within days, but the bath incident was absorbed into a much larger attempted-murder case and the prosecution then suffered years of delay. No reliable source I found identifies a first officer's interpretation or a preserved electrical component. For fiction, the instructive point is not the missing technical detail but the victim's realization that an ordinary act inside her own bathroom had been made lethal by the person caring for her. Maria da Penha, Inter-American Commission on Human Rights

3. In Lima, Ohio, in 2006, anesthesiologist Mark Wangler killed his wife, Kathy Wangler, with carbon monoxide while they slept in separate rooms of their house. He maintained that a defective water heater had caused an accident, but investigators found the appliances operating normally, soot associated with the register serving Kathy's room, and molecular tracers in duct soot that experts associated with engine exhaust. The method relied on knowing that Kathy slept upstairs while Mark slept downstairs with ventilation available to him. The first fire personnel treated the scene as a carbon-monoxide emergency, not an obvious homicide; a deficient water-heater flue gave the accident explanation some initial plausibility. Police did investigate, but the decisive duct and soot work required later search warrants and laboratory analysis, and Mark was not indicted until 2009. The exact apparatus used to introduce exhaust into the system is UNVERIFIED in the appellate judgment. For fiction, this case demonstrates that an apparently genuine household defect can coexist with one anomalous physical distribution pattern that only becomes meaningful slowly. State v. Wangler, Ohio Court of Appeals

4. In Washington State in 1991, insurance salesman Joseph Meling tried to kill his wife, Jennifer, with a cyanide-laced Sudafed capsule. He persuaded the healthy Jennifer to take it by telling her she sounded congested and snored, then later planted other poisoned Sudafed packages in shops to make her poisoning appear to be random product tampering. The intimate element was his ability to create the symptom and then present the familiar domestic remedy. Jennifer survived. Doctors were initially baffled, but the 911 operator, paramedics and hospital staff found Meling's performance and his unsolicited suggestion of cyanide suspicious. The evidence ultimately included the poisoned capsules, insurance records, a cyanide purchase linked by handwriting evidence, and the pattern of planted packages. Investigators focused on him early, although building the case required lengthy surveillance. For fiction, it shows how a killer can weaponize not merely an object but the trusted recommendation that causes the victim to use it. United States v. Meling, Ninth Circuit

5. In Northumberland County, Pennsylvania, in 2019, caregiver Tina Young was accused of putting ground rodenticide into cigarettes she rolled for a 73-year-old woman and the woman's 80-year-old husband. The routine was unusually intimate because Young prepared the couple's cigarettes and knew they would smoke what she rolled. The woman noticed an unfamiliar green material in the tobacco before the source reports any confirmed serious illness. State police said Young admitted adding the rodenticide, and she was arrested; police therefore acted on the first report rather than dismissing it. The cigarettes and visible green particles were the physical evidence, although the available contemporaneous report does not give the laboratory result or establish how reliably smoking that formulation would have caused death, so those points are UNVERIFIED. For fiction, the useful feature is that the victim detects a foreign material because she knows the normal texture and colour of an object outsiders regard as dirty and variable. Tina Young cigarette case, ABC News

6. In Auburn, Washington, in 1986, Stella Nickell was convicted of killing her husband, Bruce Nickell, by placing cyanide in Excedrin capsules and then contaminating other retail bottles to make his death resemble random product tampering. Bruce habitually used the household painkiller and took capsules after coming home with a headache. Doctors initially attributed his sudden death to emphysema; another adult's death from a cyanide-laced Excedrin bottle caused authorities to reconsider. The evidence included cyanide in the capsules and Bruce's remains, multiple contaminated bottles connected to Stella, insurance evidence and later circumstantial evidence concerning her research. The first medical interpretation was natural death, and no immediate police action followed until the second poisoning exposed the supposed product fault. For fiction, this case shows how convincingly an intimate murder can hide inside a widely manufactured object until repetition destroys the coincidence. Stella and Bruce Nickell, CBS News

7. At Doonan on Australia's Sunshine Coast in 2016, engineer Robert Ridgeway tried to kill his wife, Pepita Ridgeway, after she moved from their marital bedroom into a caravan on the same property. He ran a hose from a nitrogen cylinder to the space beneath her pillow and drilled holes beneath the mattress through which the gas could enter while she slept. He knew exactly where she now slept and that she would be alone there. Pepita woke because a persistent gurgling or hissing sound did not behave like the rats she first suspected; she lifted the mattress and found ten drilled holes, black tape, the hose and the cylinder. Police questioned Ridgeway immediately but released him, and he remained free for about a month before arrest. The apparatus was unmistakable once exposed, although Pepita said an undiscovered nitrogen death could have resembled a heart problem or stroke. For fiction, the case proves that persistent, rhythmically wrong sound can let an attentive victim discover a lethal preparation that would otherwise leave little toxic trace. Robert and Pepita Ridgeway, ABC News

8. A 1970 British forensic-science case report by D. V. Baldock describes a woman who attempted to murder her husband by filling his bedroom with bottled Calor gas. A blood sample taken about six hours after he was removed still permitted gas-chromatography detection of the fuel's constituents. The accessible abstract establishes the marital relationship, bedroom exposure, survival and physical blood evidence, but it does not give the couple's names, precise location, apparatus, husband's sleeping routine, discovery circumstances or first police reaction. Those points are therefore UNVERIFIED, and it would be misleading to invent a loose fitting, hose or valve. For fiction, it is instructive because it shows that a bedroom gas incident can initially resemble an ordinary leak while still leaving a delayed chemical signature. D. V. Baldock, "Attempted Murder by Calor Gas"

9. This is an evidential boundary case. At Willoughton, Lincolnshire, in 2007, prosecutors alleged that Ian Price set a heat gun on a timer in the spare room of the home he shared with his wife, Joanne Price, with nearby equipment intended to enlarge the resulting fire. Joanne awoke and called emergency services after the fire began. The prosecution theory relied on her sleeping at home while Price was elsewhere, but no more specific private routine is confirmed. The heat gun, timer and fire origin were physical evidence that immediately suggested deliberate preparation rather than wear, although the available report does not describe the first firefighter's interpretation. Price was later acquitted of attempted murder, so attribution and murderous intent must be marked UNVERIFIED despite the documented fire and apparatus. For fiction, the pattern is useful, but the acquittal is a warning that an apparently sinister arrangement may still fail to prove who assembled it or why. Ian and Joanne Price, Irish Examiner

## Part two: what the real world teaches, applied

1. This descends from Baldock's Calor-gas case. Before making her fried egg after a swim, Violet sees that the tiny paint line she once put across the cooker connection no longer meets itself; she made the line after a repair because she cannot tolerate two edges that almost align. She does not strike the match. The daily egg, her arrival cold enough to hurry, and her practice of lighting that particular old cooker are known only to the four, each of whom could have taken the spare key. With the supply isolated, she photographs a connection sitting one flat away from her mark, with fresh bright metal inside old black grease: turned, not fractured. A gas fitter says old connections loosen, her paint may have crept, and nobody can date a wrench mark; an officer sees a maintenance defect and no identifiable intruder. She keeps the photographs and, after replacement, the marked fitting in a sealed envelope. It fails if the period gas is so strongly odorised that every reader expects her to smell it long before a lethal concentration forms.

2. This descends from Peter Ellis's bath electrocution attempt. Violet comes home wet, sits at the kitchen table that evening and notices that the brass base of her layout lamp is no longer centred on the rectangular shadow in the paint that has surrounded it for years. She reaches for the wall switch rather than the lamp and leaves it disconnected. The four know that she works late beneath that metal lamp, often not long after swimming, and that she constantly moves the paper but never the lamp. Inside the base she finds one terminal screw cleanly backed out and bright copper lying against the brass where grime shows it did not lie before; nothing is burnt or broken. An electrician calls it a loose antique terminal and says vibration, heat cycles or earlier amateur work could explain both the screw and clean copper, while an officer cannot prove anyone entered or intended harm. Violet photographs the undisturbed interior, then keeps the entire disconnected lamp. It fails if the electrical path requires so many favourable circumstances that readers regard death as possible but not intended.

3. This descends from the Maria da Penha bath case. Returning from the harbour, Violet notices that one screw slot on the old shower control is horizontal although she aligned both slots vertically when she painted the bathroom. She cuts power before touching the fitting and finds that a single conductor has been moved from its proper terminal to the metal terminal, with fresh screwdriver crescents in the dust. Her rapid hot shower after a cold swim is known only to the four, as is her habit of entering it still wet and impatient. To her, the changed screw and the clean arc through settled dust prove recent hands; to an electrician, they prove only that an old fitting was dangerously miswired at some unknown date, and an officer cannot establish who opened it. She photographs the screw slots, dust and conductor before the electrician makes it safe, and keeps the replaced control plate. It fails if the electrician is so certain that this particular misconnection cannot be accidental that the police would have to treat it as deliberate sabotage.

4. This descends from State v. Wangler. Violet is about to run the old gas water heater after her swim when she notices that the soot feather above its inspection opening bends left, although it has always risen straight through a line she painted on the wall. She shuts it down and finds the removable internal baffle sitting backwards, with a clean semicircle showing where it had rested correctly until recently. Only the four know that she runs the heater hard before five-thirty, then sometimes lies down and sleeps deeply after the cold water. A gas fitter says an incorrectly seated baffle is common after servicing and cannot say whether it shifted, was replaced badly or was turned deliberately; an officer hears "old appliance" and has no offence or suspect. Violet photographs the soot, the clean semicircle and the baffle in place, then keeps the removed baffle after replacement. This is a single-tampering cousin of the rejected blocked-vent plot because nothing else is disabled and the danger comes from one displaced component. It fails if readers see any carbon-monoxide heater incident as the same television murder despite the single alteration and Violet's visual discovery.

5. This descends from Robert Ridgeway's gas-to-the-sleeping-place pattern. Violet returns from swimming and reaches to light the small bedroom gas fire before getting under the blankets; she stops because the painted rose around its service fitting has shifted enough to make one side of the border visibly thicker. Inspection shows that the capped fitting has been deliberately turned within an old bed of paint, leaving a new crescent of bare brass and an unbroken thread. All four know she lights this fire only after the coldest swims and then sleeps heavily, and all four know where to obtain the spare key. A fitter says old caps can move during cleaning or servicing and that the exposed brass cannot be dated accurately; an officer cannot distinguish sabotage from a neglected fitting in an old house. Violet photographs the paint crescent and keeps the replaced cap. It fails if the smell of leaking gas gives her, and the reader, too obvious a warning for the attempt to feel controlled.

6. This descends from Joseph Meling's Sudafed poisoning. I have added one small habit: after a cold swim Violet takes one old-style decongestant capsule from a bottle in her bedside drawer because salt water leaves her nose blocked, a habit all four have watched and teased her about. One morning she notices that the printed word on a capsule crosses its seam crookedly, opens it instead of swallowing it, and finds coarse unfamiliar crystals mixed with the usual powder. The four alone know the capsule, the timing and the drawer, and any of them could have used the spare key. An officer sees an opened capsule and an unidentified substance that might be manufacturing variation or contamination; without symptoms, a named suspect or laboratory work, there is no immediate criminal conclusion. Violet seals that capsule and two untouched comparison capsules separately and photographs the crooked printing. It fails if the added medicine habit feels planted solely to provide a murder mechanism.

7. This descends from Tina Young's adulterated-cigarette case. Violet takes one of her two hidden cigarettes from the kitchen cupboard and notices that its tobacco column is darker on one side and that the paper seam does not follow the maker's straight printed line. She splits it over white paper and finds several green grains placed together rather than randomly mixed through the tobacco. Only the four know she smokes, know where she hides the packet and know she always chooses the front cigarette without looking. An officer sees a hand-opened cigarette containing material that could be flavouring, mould or debris, while a tobacconist says cheap cigarettes vary and only slow laboratory work could identify the grains. Violet keeps the cigarette, grains and remaining packet sealed separately, with close photographs beside a ruler. It fails because the lethality of inhaling a particular rodenticide is less immediately convincing than the killer's apparent intention, so the mechanism risks feeling like folklore.

8. This descends from the Ian Price heat-source allegation. During late layout work, Violet switches on her familiar flexible-neck lamp and instantly sees that its oval of light cuts across her ruled border instead of sitting inside it; the lamp head has been bent down until its hot shade touches the tracing-paper stack. She unplugs it before the paper does more than discolour. The four know that she leaves that lamp burning while she washes her brushes, then goes upstairs, and that the paper stack always occupies the same squared position. In the dust on the lamp stem she finds a clean thumb-shaped break and a fresh bend beyond the point where the old neck normally stops, which tells her it was moved rather than sagged. An electrician says flexible necks droop under heat and an officer says the lamp, paper and fingerprints are all hers, with no way to prove another person bent it. Violet photographs the light pattern, dust break and brown line on the top sheet, then keeps that sheet. It fails because even if the paper caught and the terrace burned, death is less certain than in the gas, electrical and capsule candidates.

## Forced ranking

1. The marked cooker connection beats the live lamp because it joins a private daily ritual, a visually exact clue and a defect a fitter could genuinely dismiss.
2. The live layout lamp beats the displaced heater baffle because it belongs more completely to Violet's workmanlike eye and identity.
3. The heater baffle beats the shower control because the soot geometry lets Violet discover the threat without touching the endangered object.
4. The shower control beats the bedroom gas fitting because the altered screw alignment is more distinctively hers and less dependent on ignoring a smell.
5. The bedroom gas fitting beats the decongestant capsule because it belongs naturally to the established cold-swim routine without adding a habit.
6. The decongestant capsule beats the cigarette because a capsule containing a lethal adulterant has clearer real-world precedent and more believable fatality.
7. The cigarette beats the bent lamp because its intimacy and secrecy are stronger even though its toxic mechanism needs care.
8. The bent lamp is last because a fatal fire is plausible but not as inevitable, and the movement can too easily read as ordinary sag.
